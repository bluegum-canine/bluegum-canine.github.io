One-to-one training

Private training at your home, or on ground of your choosing. For most dogs that means everyday obedience: recall, settle, loose lead and stopping on command. It can just as well be one particular thing you want your dog to learn.

What it is

Why it falls apart outside

Good in the kitchen
is only the start

Almost every owner I speak to says the same thing: he knows it at home. And he usually does. What he doesn’t know yet is that the same word means the same thing on the beach, at the gate, with a cat in the hedge and with you sitting down instead of standing up.

That is why obedience is built in stages, and why the middle stages are the ones people skip. Teaching a dog what a word means should take the longest, and is almost always rushed. Reinforcing it until it is a habit, not a favour. Proofing it, which means deliberately practising around the distractions that break it, a little at a time. Maintaining it, which is the part that belongs to you, for the rest of the dog’s life.

Skip proofing and you have a dog that is obedient right up until it matters.

What we work on

Four things that
buy a dog its freedom

Recall

Coming back first time, straight to you, without a detour past the interesting smell. We start close, where your dog can hardly get it wrong, and add distance and distraction only as fast as it keeps succeeding. A long line keeps it safe in the meantime, so the dog never learns that ignoring you is an option.

Settle

Going to a spot and staying there, calmly, until you say it’s over. It is the most useful thing a family dog can know: visitors at the door, dinner on the table, the vet’s waiting room. The dog learns to switch itself off, rather than being held back.

Loose lead

A walk you actually enjoy. Pulling is not a dog being dominant; it is a dog that has learned pulling gets it where it wants to go faster. Change what pulling earns and the lead goes slack.

Stopping on command

One word that stops your dog where it is: before the road, before the sheep, before the child with the ice cream. It is the one you hope you never need, and the one worth proofing hardest.

In an emergency it is better than a recall, and the reason is geometry. Calling a dog back brings it towards you, which may mean bringing it across the very thing you are trying to keep it away from. Dropping it where it stands takes the danger out of the picture without the dog crossing anything. Then you walk to the dog.

It takes longer to build than people expect, because a down that holds at thirty feet with the dog already moving is a different behaviour from a down at your feet in the kitchen. It gets built in small steps, in that order, and the solid stay has to come first. There is no point adding distance to something that does not hold at two paces.

Or one particular goal

Same price

Not everybody wants the whole list. Sometimes there is one thing you want your dog to be able to do, and one-to-one is where that gets taught. Tell me the goal and I will tell you honestly how many sessions it is likely to take.

  • Going into a crate, and being content to stay there
  • Getting into the car, and travelling quietly once in it. More on dogs that refuse the car
  • A proper retrieve: going out, picking up, bringing it back and giving it up
  • Scent work: finding one particular smell and telling you where it is, below
  • Telling you about a sound: the phone ringing, the doorbell, a timer, an alarm. Your dog learns to come and find you when it hears it
  • A deterrent bark on command: your dog barks on your word and stops on your word. Useful at the door, and it is a bark and nothing more

If yours is not on the list, ask. If it is something I should not be teaching your dog, I will say that too.

Scent work, as an add-on

Part of a session

Not a separate service, and not an extra charge: searching is something we can put into your sessions, or spend a whole one on if you enjoy it. Boxes, containers, a room, the car. Your dog learns to find one particular smell and tell you where it is.

  • A dog works on its own, so a dog that cannot yet cope near other dogs can do it from day one, and be good at something, which matters more than it sounds
  • It tires a dog out mentally in a way a walk does not, which helps the dogs that never seem to switch off
  • It suits older, arthritic and recovering dogs, and dogs on restricted exercise
  • Started in your own house, with nothing you need to buy

How a block of sessions runs

Built around your dog,
not a syllabus

There is no fixed curriculum. Before the first session I want to know how your dog actually lives, where it sleeps, who feeds it, what a normal day looks like, what has been tried and what happened. That is where most answers are hiding.

The first session goes straight at the thing that worries you most. You should see something change on the first day, even if it is small, so you know the plan is worth working. The first session usually runs longer than the rest.

After that, sessions run an hour to an hour and a half, usually a week apart. Each one starts with how the week went, because what happens between sessions matters far more than what happens during them. Then we move the work on a step: a busier place, a longer wait, a closer distraction.

What I will ask of you

An hour or so a week with me is not going to beat the rest of the week on its own. Your dog is learning all day, from everything, so the work is mostly yours, and it is mostly small.

  • House rules, written down. What the dog is and isn’t allowed to do, agreed by everyone and stuck on the fridge. Dogs cope well with “always” and “never”. They cope very badly with “sometimes”. It is usually the first thing that changes the dog
  • The same words from everyone. If one person says “down” for lie down and another says it for get off the sofa, the dog is not being disobedient
  • A few minutes, several times a day. Short sessions that end while the dog is still keen beat an hour on Sunday
  • Watching what your dog learns on its own. Your dog is awake for most of the day and learning the whole time. The counter that sometimes has food on it teaches more than any session I run
  • Honesty about the week. If the homework didn’t happen, tell me, and we will make it smaller rather than pretend

Who one-to-one suits

  • Dogs that cannot yet cope near other dogs, so a class isn’t fair on them or on you
  • Problems that only happen in one place: your gate, your car, your road, your visitors
  • Owners who want to move faster than a group allows, or who would simply rather not have an audience
  • Anything that is starting to frighten you. That usually begins with a behaviour consultation and carries on as a block of sessions

It is not a way to have the dog fixed without you. If you need someone else to do the heavy lifting first, residential training exists for exactly that, and you are still taught at the end of it.

Common questions

How many sessions will we need? I will tell you honestly once I have met your dog. It depends on the dog, the habits already there and how much practice happens between sessions. Most behaviour work is a block of six; a single everyday problem can take fewer.

Is my dog too old? No. Older dogs learn perfectly well; they may simply have more habits to undo first.

Where do we train? At home first, where the habits are formed, then wherever the problem actually happens.

Should the whole family be there? Whoever walks and feeds the dog, at least. A dog that listens to one person is only partly trained.

Can I do it in a class instead? Yes, if your dog copes around others, see group classes. One-to-one suits problems that happen in one place, or a dog that isn’t ready for a room full of dogs.
