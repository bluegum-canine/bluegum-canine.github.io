Working dogs

An area I trained in properly and know well — but not a service I am offering right now. Questions welcome, by email.

Where I stand

Not offered

A working dog is one with a job, and there are two kinds of them. A single-purpose dog searches — detection and scent work, and nothing else. A dual-purpose dog searches as well, and is trained for protection on top of it: patrol, apprehension, and the handler’s safety. The difference between the two →

I do not train protection dogs, and I am not going to. Not because I cannot, but because the risk it carries and the resources it needs are both out of proportion to anything it would add here. If that is what you are looking for, I am not your man, and I would rather say so on the page than after a phone call. What the training is worth to you is further down this page, and it is not what people expect.

Detection is one of the things Highland Canine is best known for, and I trained in it under Jason Purgason, who runs the school and whose own research the method comes from. It was a real part of my qualification: 58 hours on training detection dogs, inside a 234-hour block of working-dog modules, and time in the field with young dogs at the very start of their training.

A dog can be taught to find almost anything that gives off a smell, and most of the useful work has nothing to do with either of those.

For pet dogs, searching is an activity rather than a job — and one of the few a nervous or reactive dog can do well, because it works on its own. That part I do offer, inside ordinary one-to-one sessions.

The short version

How a dog learns
a smell

Before a detection dog is asked to search for anything, it is taught to love one smell.

That comes first, on its own, with no searching and no job attached. Highland’s method is exact about it: at least 75 minutes of contact with each smell, one at a time, before the next is added. The method is Jason’s and the school’s, not mine, and he is happy for it to be known.

Seventy-five minutes sounds easy until you count it properly. It only counts while the dog’s nose is right at the smell. A dog sniffing a row of boxes gets a fraction of a second at each one, so the time is built up another way, with the smell carried in a toy the dog plays with.

What a dog can smell also depends less on how much of something there is than on how much of it is exposed to the air. A cut onion fills a kitchen. A whole one in the cupboard barely registers. The same rule decides how hard a find is.

Working dogs

Single-purpose and
dual-purpose dogs

Not every working dog has the same job, and the job decides how the dog is trained — including how much it is allowed to pull.

A single-purpose dog does one thing: it searches. A dual-purpose dog searches too, but is also trained for protection work, such as patrol or apprehension. At Highland the two are taught differently, and the difference starts with control.

The protection half of that is not the sport version. Competition protection — IGP, ringsport and the rest — is a demanding discipline with real standards behind it, but it is still a sport: the helper is known, the exercise is agreed in advance, and a clever dog learns the pattern as much as the job. Operational bite work is trained for a situation nobody has arranged, against someone who has not agreed to be there, and it is judged on what happens on the worst day rather than on what scores well on a good one.

That is why the control standard is so much higher on this side. A dog that can be called off, held on to and put away is the whole precondition — without that, it is not training, it is a liability on a lead.

What this is worth to a pet dog owner

None of it is work I offer. It still earns its keep every time I assess a dog, and here is the honest reason why.

Two dogs can do the identical thing on the end of a lead — hard forward, noisy, fixed on something — for opposite reasons. One is a dog with real prey drive, doing enthusiastically what it was bred to do. The other is a dog that has had too much pressure put on it and has learned that going forward loudly makes the frightening thing go away. A third, under the same pressure, does the reverse and shuts down: goes quiet, goes flat, stops offering anything. That one is routinely read as a good dog having a nice calm day, and it is nothing of the sort.

Telling those apart is the whole job, because the plans are opposites. Drive wants an outlet and a way to switch it off. Pressure wants the pressure taken off first, and anything that adds more will make it worse however obedient it looks in the moment.

Protection training is where you learn to see the difference, because it is the one place a dog is put under pressure and brought into drive deliberately, on purpose, with people around who know exactly where the line is. You watch dogs cross it and you watch them handled back from it. After that you recognise both from across a car park, which is usually where I first see them.

Single-purpose Dual-purpose
The job Searching, and nothing else Searching, plus patrol, apprehension or protection
Control Looser. Tight, competition-style heelwork is not needed and gets in the way Much stricter, because the dog must be safe to hold on to when the other work switches on
What matters most Freedom to pull the handler towards the smell; step in only for real hazards such as a road or broken glass Handler control first

Why it matters at home

A pet dog that enjoys searching is single-purpose in everything but name. It does not need to be held to a strict heelwork standard to search well. What matters most for single-purpose dogs such as Labradors and pointers is a reliable recall, and a dog that has learned it is allowed to follow its nose.

Obedience and searching are best taught separately. A dog drilled in strict obedience at the thing it is trying to find can learn to switch its nose off. Teach the sit on its own first, then bring it into the search later.

Anatomy

Why a dog’s nose
beats ours

We see the world. A dog smells it — and the difference is built into the shape of its head.

Person Dog
Scent receptors About 5 to 6 million About 200 to 300 million, depending on breed
Scent-detecting lining A patch roughly the size of a postage stamp Spread over a maze of thin, scrolled bones — about the size of a postcard if laid flat, twenty times ours
Brain given over to smell A small part A far larger share, for the size of the brain
Sensitivity The baseline Commonly put at tens of thousands of times ours

Smelling and breathing are kept apart

When we breathe in, the air rushes past a small patch of scent lining high in the nose, and most of it goes straight to the lungs. A dog’s nose splits each breath. A fold of tissue sends a portion of it into a separate chamber at the back of the nose, used only for smelling, where scent can build up instead of being swept away.

In and out at the same time

Look at a dog’s nostrils and you will see a slit at the side of each one. Air breathed out leaves through the slits, sideways and backwards, so it does not blow away the scent the dog is about to draw in — and it stirs up more from the ground as it goes. A dog working a smell takes short, rapid sniffs, several a second, and can keep fresh air coming in more or less continuously.

Smelling in stereo

A dog can move and sample each nostril separately, which lets it tell which side a smell is stronger on. That is how a tracking dog knows which way a trail runs, and how a searching dog narrows down to the source. The wet surface of the nose helps too, by catching scent from the air.

A second sense in the roof of the mouth

Behind the front teeth, in the roof of the mouth, dogs have the vomeronasal organ (also called Jacobson’s organ), linked straight to the part of the brain that handles smell. It is part of how dogs read each other’s scent signals, and in effect it lets them taste a smell. It is one reason a dog carrying a scented toy in its mouth learns that smell so thoroughly.

What a nose cannot do

No nose finds what is not in the air. A smell has to lift off its source to be found, and some substances are made of molecules too heavy to do that on their own. As a rule of thumb taught at Highland, anything with a molecular weight much over 300 stays put.

Heroin is the clearest example. Its molecular weight is about 369, so a drug dog is not really smelling heroin at all — it is smelling the lighter chemicals that come off it, and the main one is acetic acid. Household vinegar is five per cent acetic acid.

Jason and his colleagues once put that to the test with hundreds of police and military dog teams in Mexico, all trained on the same commercial training aid. Without telling the handlers, they set out aspirin, talc, a binder and a splash of vinegar. About 97 per cent of the dogs indicated as if they had found heroin.

The dogs were not wrong. They found exactly what they had been taught to find. It is why what a dog is trained on matters as much as the dog. (For clarity: I do not do drugs or explosives work. It is simply the best illustration there is.)

The equipment is already there. Training is what turns it into a dog that tells you, reliably, what it has found.

Closer to home

Your own dog’s nose

You do not need a working dog to use any of this. Searching for a smell is quiet, steady work that tires a dog out more than a long walk does. It can be done in a kitchen or a back field, with no other dogs around.

That makes it useful for dogs who find the outside world hard: nervous dogs, dogs who react on walks, and rescues still settling in. Where it will help, it can be part of a behaviour plan.

Scent work was a real part of my training at Highland Canine, and it is something I can teach your dog, and you, properly — as an activity in one-to-one sessions, or as part of a behaviour plan.

It does not have to stay in the kitchen, either. Jessie, my yellow Labrador, has started trailing: she watches a friend run off into the woods, then has to find them by nose alone, wearing a harness on a thirty-foot line. She took to it brilliantly.

Nothing quite beats it. You have no idea where the person has gone, so you have to trust your dog and, quite literally, follow their lead. When they find someone you could never have found yourself, it is a real thrill, and you will both have a lot of fun getting there.
