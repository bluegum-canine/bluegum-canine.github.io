Most of this page is about one thing: why the cause matters more than the behaviour. If you are reading it because your dog has growled at somebody, it is worth ten minutes.

Why the cause decides everything

Two dogs can do the same
thing for opposite reasons

A dog barks and lunges at the end of the lead. Another dog, same street, same week, does something that looks identical. One is frightened and wants the other dog to go away. The other is thoroughly confident and wants to get at it.

They look the same to almost everyone watching. They are not the same problem, and what helps one will usually make the other worse. That is the whole argument for assessing a dog properly before anybody starts training it.

This is also why advice from the internet, from a class, or from a well-meaning neighbour so often fails. The advice is not necessarily wrong. It is answering a different question about a different dog.

So the first job is never “how do we stop this?” It is what is this dog actually trying to achieve? Get that right and the plan writes itself. Get it wrong and you can spend six months making a dog worse while doing everything you were told.

What is actually going on

The kinds of aggression,
and why they differ

These are not neat boxes. Real dogs turn up with two or three of them at once, and part of the assessment is working out which one is driving the rest.

Fear

By far the most common, and the one most often missed. Fear aggression has one purpose: to make something go away. Growling, barking, showing teeth, lunging — all of it is the dog asking for distance, and it works, because the person or the dog usually does back off. Every time it works, it becomes a better strategy.

These dogs are often perfectly fine at home. The owner is genuinely shocked the first time it happens somewhere else.

Guarding something

Food, a bone, a toy, a stolen sock, a person, the sofa. The dog is not frightened of you — it has something it values and has learned that a hard stare or a growl keeps it. The work is about changing what the dog expects to happen when you approach, and about controlling access to the thing, not about winning a confrontation over it.

Territory and space

The doorway, the garden, the van, the kennel run. Behaviour that never appears on a walk can be severe in a confined space, with nothing to guard and nobody in danger. If you have a dog that is civil everywhere except its own gate, this is usually why.

Rules and social pressure

A smaller group than the internet suggests, but real: dogs that have learned that a warning ends an argument — being moved off the bed, having a collar taken hold of, being asked to do something they would rather not. It is often the most misdiagnosed category in both directions, and it tends to resolve faster than fear once it is correctly identified.

Chase

Sheep, cattle, cats, joggers, cars. This one is not anger and not fear — it is hunting, it is deeply satisfying to the dog, and it rehearses itself every time it happens. It is also the one with legal consequences attached in this part of the country. Livestock worrying has its own page.

Frustration, and pain

A dog that desperately wants to get to something and cannot — on a lead, behind a fence — can look ferocious and be nothing of the sort. And a dog that hurts somewhere will defend the part that hurts. A behaviour that arrives suddenly, in a dog that was fine last month, goes to your vet before it comes to me. More cases than people expect turn out to be a sore hip, an ear, a thyroid or failing eyesight.

Most dogs I see are more than one of these at once. The question is which one is in charge.

The one that is different

Why fear is never
worked the same way

If a dog is frightened, everything that would be reasonable with a confident dog becomes a mistake.

Pressure makes it worse. A frightened dog that is corrected for growling learns that the thing it was worried about now predicts trouble from you as well. You have added a second fear to the first, and you have done it at the exact moment the dog was asking for help.

You can lose the warning. Growling is not the problem — it is the dog telling you what it is about to do. Punish it often enough and some dogs stop growling and go straight to the bite. Those are the dogs people describe as having “no warning at all”, and they were usually made that way.

Flooding does not work. Standing a frightened dog in the middle of the thing it fears until it gives up is not learning. The dog has shut down. Take it home, do it again next week, and you will find the fear has grown.

What actually changes a frightened dog is changing what it expects to happen — at a distance it can cope with, many times, until the trigger genuinely predicts something good, and then closing the distance by inches. It is unglamorous and it is slow. Fear is one of the most reliably fixable causes and one of the slowest: think months, not a fortnight. Anyone who promises you a frightened dog fixed in a session either has a different dog or is not telling you the truth.

Getting the trigger right

“He’s scared of men”
is rarely the answer

Fear is usually far more specific than the description of it. Not men, but men in high-vis. Not dogs, but black dogs that stare. Not slippery floors, but one particular pattern of floor. Not noise, but one noise the dog heard once on a very bad day.

This matters because the work is done at the trigger. Train against the wrong one and you can practise diligently for months and change nothing — the dog was never frightened of what you were practising with.

So the assessment happens where the behaviour happens, not in a hall, and I will usually ask for video of the real thing before I form a view. Ten seconds of footage of your dog at the gate is worth more than an hour of either of us describing it.

If I cannot work out what it is, I say so — and I tell you who to ask next.
