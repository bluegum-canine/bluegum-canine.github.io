Assistance dogs

An area I trained in properly and know well — but not a service I am offering right now. Questions welcome, by email.

Where I stand

Not offered

Task training for mobility, autism, signal work for deaf handlers, and dogs for seizures and PTSD was the largest single block of my qualification — 334 hours — and it is work that genuinely interests me. It is not a service I offer. An assistance dog is a multi-year commitment for the family as much as the dog, and it deserves to be run properly or not at all.

What I can be is a source of information, and some gentle guidance if you need it — whether you are thinking about an assistance dog, wondering whether a dog you already have might suit, or working out where you stand on access. Ireland and the UK have no register, no certificate and no legal category called a service dog, which surprises most people. What the law actually says about assistance dogs →

Before you start looking

The honest version,
from the start

These are the things I would want a family to know before they spend a euro or fall in love with a puppy.

It takes longer than people expect

From choosing a dog to a dog that can reliably work in public is commonly eighteen months to two years, and longer for complicated tasks. It does not stop there. The training has to be kept up for the whole of the dog’s working life, and that work falls to the family.

The dog has to suit the person who will handle it

A dog that is easy for a professional can be too much for a child, or for someone with limited grip or balance. The right question is not “is this a good dog?” but “can the person who will live with it control it on a bad day?” A lot of high-energy, clever dogs fail on exactly that.

Temperament matters more than breed

There is no special assistance-dog breed. Crossbreeds and small dogs have done the work well. What matters is a dog that is keen to work for food, toys or praise, is relaxed with strangers, and recovers quickly from a sudden noise or a slippery floor. Every dog gets a fright; the ones that suit shake it off.

Two cautions. Very large, short-lived breeds give a family few working years after a long training period. And when choosing a puppy, look at each one on its own — a nervous puppy can borrow confidence from a bold littermate and look better than it is.

The dog you already have

People often ask whether the family dog could do it. Sometimes, yes, and a dog that is already bonded to the family has a real head start. But most pet dogs are not suited to the pressure of public work, and that is no criticism of the dog. A dog with a fear problem is “not yet” rather than “no” — but it needs that problem dealt with first.

If you are talking to a provider

Questions worth
asking anyone

  • Who owns the dog? Some programmes keep ownership and can take the dog back. That may be fine, but know it before you commit
  • How is the dog chosen, and are the selection criteria written down?
  • What happens if the dog doesn’t make it? Some dogs are withdrawn during training. Ask what that means for you and for the money
  • What support is there afterwards, and for how long?
  • Who does the day-to-day training, and what will the family be expected to do?

And be clear on the difference between an assistance dog, a therapy dog that visits hospitals or schools, and a companion that simply helps someone feel better at home. They are trained differently and the law treats them differently — the law page sets that out.
