Assistance dogs

The law
at the door

People arrive at this question expecting an Irish version of the American rules, and there isn't one. There is no certificate to hold, no register to appear on, and no legal category called a “service dog”. What there is instead is worth understanding properly, because it decides what happens when somebody is refused entry to a shop.

I have set out below how Ireland, the UK and the United States each handle this, side by side. The 334 hours of assistance work on my Master Trainer programme are the largest single block of it, and this is the part people ask about before anything else.

In the US the right attaches to the dog. In Ireland and the UK it attaches to the person — the dog is simply the accommodation they are owed.

That one difference explains nearly everything else. Because Irish and British law protect the disabled person rather than certifying the animal, there is nothing for a handler to produce at the door and nothing for a shop assistant to check. The upside is that no dog type is second class: a mobility dog stands exactly where a guide dog stands. The downside is that disputes get settled after the fact, by complaint, rather than on the day.

The question people actually ask

Which kinds of dog
the law names

Guide dogs are the only type with real recognition on this side of the Atlantic, and that recognition is social rather than statutory. The one place British law does distinguish between dog types is the taxi rank.

The list of “prescribed charities” in the UK taxi provisions is short and inherited from the pre-2010 Disability Discrimination Act era — Dogs for the Disabled, now Dogs for Good, along with Support Dogs and Canine Partners. Section 173 contains a power to prescribe further kinds of disability which has barely been used. A perfectly capable owner-trained mobility dog therefore gets no criminal-law backstop at the taxi rank.

Jurisdiction notes

What each system
feels like in practice

Ireland

Equal Status Acts 2000–2018

  • No accreditation gatekeeper. No register, no licence, and no requirement that any named organisation trained the dog.
  • Refusing entry or charging a cleaning surcharge to a handler is discrimination, full stop.
  • Enforcement is slow and compensation-only. Serve an ES.1 notice within two months, then file with the WRC.
  • The law is not self-enforcing. Irish Guide Dogs found in 2023 that 83% of guide and assistance dog owners had a negative access experience in the previous twelve months.
  • Charities are lobbying for assistance dogs other than guide dogs to get specific statutory recognition. Nothing has been enacted.

United Kingdom

Equality Act 2010 · DDA 1995 in Northern Ireland

  • Two regimes, not one. Services run on the reasonable-adjustment duty and are entirely dog-type agnostic.
  • Taxis and private hire are the exception — a narrow statutory definition, and a criminal offence for drivers who refuse or surcharge.
  • Drivers can be exempted on medical grounds, but must display the exemption notice.
  • Northern Ireland still runs on the DDA 1995, so check it separately for anything crossing the border.
  • An ADUK book is evidence, not a licence. Useful at the door; legally never required.

United States

ADA 1990 · Fair Housing Act · ACAA

  • One national definition covering any disability, tied to trained work or tasks rather than to a diagnosis or a provider.
  • Staff have a script — two permitted questions, no documentation, no demonstration of the task.
  • Miniature horses get a separate accommodation provision. Every other species is out.
  • Emotional support animals are excluded from public access, though the Fair Housing Act still covers them in housing.
  • Removal is still allowed if the dog is out of control or not house-trained.

Practice notes

Where the paperwork
actually bites

The law says no documentation is needed. The world disagrees, and it is better to know where before you start.

Air and sea travel

This is the hard wall. Carriers overwhelmingly require Assistance Dogs International or IGDF accreditation, and an owner-trained dog with no ADUK or ADI book will simply be refused carriage. Nothing in the Equality Act or the Equal Status Acts obliges an airline to seat a dog in the cabin.

Hotels and hospitals

Large chains and hospital trusts run on internal policy, and internal policy asks for the book. Handlers without one win these arguments eventually, but rarely on the day.

Food premises

Nothing in Irish or UK food hygiene law bars a dog from a café or a restaurant. “Health and safety” and “the food inspector wouldn't allow it” are the two commonest refusals, and both are wrong.

Training is unregulated

Neither Ireland nor the UK licenses assistance dog trainers. Anyone may train and place one. ADI and Assistance Dogs Europe accreditation is the de facto professional standard, and it is what decides whether a handler can ultimately fly, sail or check into a hotel with the dog. I would rather say that plainly at the start than have somebody find it out at an airport.

Dogs in training

Trainee dogs have weaker standing everywhere. Some US states extend access rights to trainers by statute; Ireland and the UK generally do not, so access for a dog in training rests on the goodwill of the venue.

The limit that is the same everywhere

No jurisdiction protects a dog that is out of control, not toilet-trained, or a genuine hygiene or safety risk. Removal on those grounds is lawful in Ireland, the UK and the US alike — and it must be about the dog's actual behaviour, never the handler's condition. Which is, in the end, an argument for training the dog properly.
