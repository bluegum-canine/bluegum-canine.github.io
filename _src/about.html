About

Twenty years with dogs.
960 hours learning why

Most trainers will tell you how long they have been doing this. Very few can tell you where they were taught, for how long, or by whom — and that is the part you can actually check.

I spent more than twenty years around dogs before I had a certificate to point at. It was enough to be useful and not enough to be reliable, and I knew the difference.

In 2026 I put that right, and completed the Master Trainer Program at the Highland Canine School for Dog Trainers in North Carolina — a six-month residential programme, 960 hours, covering obedience, behaviour modification, aggression, scent and detection work, and the business of training itself. Residential meaning I left home and lived on campus for the duration, working dogs every day rather than studying them at a distance. The certificate is dated December 2026.

It was worth doing for a reason I did not expect. Two decades of experience teaches you what works; the formal training teaches you why it works, and that turns out to matter enormously when a dog does not respond the way experience says it should. Knowing the mechanism is what lets you change the plan instead of repeating it louder.

I have been a full member of the International Association of Canine Professionals since 2025, and I hold to ethical, science-led best practice. In plain terms: I will tell you what I am doing and why, I will tell you honestly whether I can help, and I will not sell you a programme your dog does not need.

The name

Why Bluegum

A blue gum is a eucalypt. I am Australian, so the name is simply where I am from — but it turned out to describe the work better than anything I could have invented.

I grew up in Australia, spent twenty-six years living and working in the UK, and came to Ireland in 2026. A blue gum has made much the same journey, and it does well here — the mild, wet Atlantic seaboard suits it, and there are eucalypts standing in gardens the length of the west and south-west. An Australian tree that is not native to Ireland, and is perfectly at home in it.

The part I like best is what a eucalypt does after a fire. It does not start again from a seed. Beneath the bark it carries thousands of dormant buds — a tall tree can hold several thousand, each one marking where a leaf used to be — and a swollen woody base that stores everything needed to push new growth. Burn it back and it comes again from what was already there.

That is close to what I actually do. Most of the dogs I am called out to are not blank slates and they are not lost causes. The groundwork is usually there, under everything that has gone wrong, waiting on the right conditions. My job is far more often to find it than to build it from nothing.

Which is the other reason the name stuck: a dog that looks burnt is not a dog that is finished.

Where I work

I am based in Sligo, Co. Sligo, and I work in rings out from there rather than to a list of counties.

Within about forty-five minutes — Sligo, Boyle, Carrick-on-Shannon, Ballymote, Collooney, Ballaghaderreen and everything between them — travel is simply part of the fee. Out to about an hour and a quarter, covering the rest of Sligo, Leitrim and Roscommon along with Longford, east Mayo and west Cavan, I come to you as a matter of course; it just helps if we can put two visits on the same day.

Beyond that I still travel, and regularly do — Castlebar, Athlone, Letterkenny, Galway are all inside a couple of hours. For those I either add something for the road or, more often, we talk about residential instead, which usually suits the dog better anyway.

And some of what I do has no map at all. Residential training, assistance dog work and written behaviour assessments are taken from anywhere in the country — for those, distance is my problem rather than yours.

Residential training is taken at home in small numbers, and owners are welcome to come and see where their dog will be staying before committing to anything. I would encourage it.
