The method

Two principles

Everything below follows from these two. If you only read one part of this site, read this part.

01

Freedom through obedience

A dog that will recall, settle and walk on a loose lead is a dog that gets to come with you. Obedience is not about control — it is what buys a dog its liberty. The best-trained dogs I know are also the ones with the most freedom, and that is not a coincidence.

02

Treat the cause,
not the symptom

A dog barks, then growls, then shows its teeth, and only then bites. That sequence is the warning system. Punish the growl and the growling stops — but the fear underneath it has not moved, and the next time there is no warning at all. That is where the “dog that bit out of nowhere” comes from.

So the first job is always working out why, and being specific about it. Not “he is reactive” but what he is reacting to, and what it has been getting him. A plan built on the wrong reason does not simply fail to help — it usually makes things worse, and quickly.

On quick fixes

Why this takes
the time it takes

You will find programmes that promise a reactive dog turned around in a few days. Sometimes the dog really does look transformed at the end of one. The question worth asking is what was actually changed.

A short programme puts the trainer under a clock. The fastest way to show visible progress against a deadline is to suppress what the owner can see — the barking, the lunging, the growling — and the fastest way to suppress those is to punish them. The dog goes quiet. The reason it was barking is untouched.

That is how a dog ends up biting “out of nowhere”. It didn’t. It ran out of warnings, because somebody took them away.

Changing what a dog genuinely believes about the thing it is afraid of takes time, and it takes you doing the work between sessions. I would rather tell you that at the start than sell you a fortnight and leave you with a quieter dog that is no safer.

Balanced and bespoke

Built for your dog,
not its breed

I use a balanced approach, and I will say so plainly rather than hide it behind a label. That means reward and consequence both have a place, and the judgement is in knowing which the situation calls for.

No two dogs arrive with the same history of what has paid off for them. A behaviour that looks irrational nearly always makes sense once you know what it has been earning the dog — and that history is individual, not breed-typical. So the plan is built for the dog in front of me.

Correction is reserved for behaviours with real safety consequences — biting, bolting, chasing stock. Nuisance habits are better solved by changing the setup, and reaching for correction on those is both unkind and ineffective.

Where correction is used, the target is a dog that quietly chooses not to do the thing, while staying confident and willing. A dog that has shut down is not a trained dog, and I would count that as a failure.

Ethical, science-led, and honest about what it is.

The stepped approach

Four steps

Every behaviour goes through the same four steps. Skip one and it will not hold — and almost every stalled training problem I see is a step that was skipped.

  1. 01

    Teaching

    Showing the dog what the behaviour actually is. This should take the longest and it is the step most often rushed. A dog that has not properly learned a behaviour cannot be said to be disobeying it.

  2. 02

    Reinforcing

    Consistent consequences, applied until the behaviour is reliable rather than optional. This is where the reward gets thinned out, which is exactly what makes the behaviour durable.

  3. 03

    Proofing

    Distance, duration and distraction — until it holds in a three-ring circus. A sit that works in the kitchen and fails at the farm gate has not been proofed, it has been practised.

  4. 04

    Maintaining

    Keeping it there for the rest of the dog’s life. This is the step where I am teaching you rather than the dog, and if I haven’t transferred it to you then I haven’t finished the job.

One thing that explains a lot

Always, never,
and sometimes

Dogs cope well with always. They cope well with never. They cope very badly with sometimes.

A great deal of training that stalls is really a household that has not yet agreed the rules. If the dog is allowed on the sofa when one person is in and not another, it is not being stubborn — it is being asked to learn a rule that does not exist. We sort the people out first, and the dog usually follows without much argument.

The same arithmetic applies to time. A dog is awake for something like eighteen hours a day. An hour a week of training is a rounding error against that; what shapes your dog is the other seventeen hours, every day. Which is why so much of what I do is about the setup at home rather than the session itself.

Two things I’ll say early

Before we start

Health before behaviour

A sudden change in behaviour with no obvious cause goes to your vet before it comes to me. Pain and illness produce behaviour that looks exactly like a training problem, and training a dog that is hurting is both useless and unfair.

It often looks worse before it looks better

A behaviour on its way out tends to spike first — harder, louder, more often — because it used to work and the dog is trying it once more. That spike is a sign of progress, and it is exactly where most people give up. I would rather warn you now than explain it afterwards.
