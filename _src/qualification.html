Dog training is unregulated in Ireland. Anyone may call themselves a trainer or a behaviourist, and most of what you will read on the subject is somebody's opinion with a number of years attached to it. So here is exactly what I was taught, where, and for how long.

Master Trainer Program, Highland Canine School for Dog Trainers, Harmony, North Carolina. Six months residential, living on campus, working dogs every day. Certificate dated December 2026.

412 hours in the classroom. 548 hours with dogs. The lab hours are the larger half deliberately — you cannot learn this from a lecture, and you cannot learn it properly from a weekend either.

Who taught it

Jason Purgason

The primary instructor at the School for Dog Trainers, President of Highland Canine Training, and current President of the International Association of Canine Professionals.

Thirty years working with dogs. A former police officer and police K9 handler who has run handler and instructor courses for law enforcement and military organisations, and trained more than a thousand dog teams in the United States and abroad.

I mention him for a specific reason. When you engage a trainer you are buying their judgement, and judgement is inherited — it comes from whoever taught them and how closely. Six months on campus is not a weekend seminar and it is not a correspondence course. It is being in the same field as your instructor, most days, being corrected.

That is the part I would want to know about, if I were hiring. Not that a certificate exists, but who stood over the work while it was being learned.

Field work, not classroom

How a dog is taught
to know a smell

Most people assume a detection dog is taught to search. It is taught something else first, and the order is the whole method.

Before a dog is asked to find anything, it is imprinted — given a purely positive association with one target odour and nothing else. No searching, no indication, no job. The standard being aimed at is odour fluency: the dog knows every chemical component of that smell, so it still recognises it in an unusually small amount, an unusually large one, or mixed with something it has never met before. A dog that only knows the odour at one strength will miss it at another.

The Highland protocol is 75 minutes of active contact per odour — and active means active. Only time the odour is genuinely in the dog's mouth or being directly inhaled is counted; carrying it, tugging on it, playing fetch with it. Not time in the room with it.

The protocol comes from a research study by Jason Purgason himself, which is the part I would not have predicted. It was field-tested last spring with handlers at Fort Bragg: the dog imprinted this way, against their usual and more classical methods, was their newest and least experienced — and the first time live hides went out, it was the best performer of the group.

The protocol is indifferent to what the odour actually is. It works on anything with a chemical signature, which is why detection dogs turn up in places most people never think to look for them — agriculture, pest control, conservation work — and not only in the two applications everybody has heard of.

Scale gives a sense of the work: a narcotics dog carries five to seven odours. An explosives dog carries at least thirteen, because explosive chemistry varies far more widely across the world and the cost of one missed odour is of a different order entirely.

None of this is needed to fix a dog that pulls on the lead. It is here because learning to build a behaviour that holds under that kind of scrutiny changes how you build the ordinary ones.

Alongside it

The other credential

I have been a full member of the International Association of Canine Professionals since 2025, which binds me to their code of conduct and to continuing professional development.

Of the trainers working in this part of Ireland, only a handful name any certifying body at all. It is worth asking anyone you are considering — not because a badge makes somebody good, but because an unwillingness to name one tells you something.
