Common problems — 03

Nervous
around people

Shrinking, barking or hiding when visitors call. This one upsets owners more than any other on the list, because it is hard to watch and easy to take personally.

What it looks like

Backing away from strangers. Barking furiously at the door and then retreating behind the sofa. Tucked tail, flattened ears, licking lips, turning away, refusing food that would normally be taken. Sometimes it presents as the opposite of nervousness — a dog that rushes forward barking — and the two are frequently the same thing wearing different clothes.

Why it happens

Fear comes from some mixture of genetics, what the dog did or did not meet between roughly three and sixteen weeks, and anything unpleasant that has happened since. None of that is a training failure or a flaw in the dog. Rescue dogs often arrive with a history nobody can reconstruct, and it is not always necessary to know it.

What keeps it going is more useful than what started it. Barking at a stranger works — the stranger goes away, or the dog retreats and the frightening thing stops. That is a behaviour being paid off, every time, and it becomes a reliable strategy the dog reaches for first.

Why the usual fixes stall

“Let them meet lots of people, they’ll get used to it.” Flooding an already-frightened dog with the thing it fears usually confirms the fear rather than curing it. Socialisation means controlled, positive exposure at a distance the dog can cope with. More contact on its own does not do that.

Asking visitors to offer a treat. This puts food directly next to the frightening thing and pulls a conflicted dog closer than it wanted to go. Dogs bite in exactly that situation, having taken the food first.

Telling the dog off for growling. The most damaging thing on this page. A growl is information, and a dog that has been punished for growling has not become less frightened — it has learned to skip the warning. That is how a dog becomes one that “bit with no warning”.

Reassurance is more complicated than either camp claims. You cannot reinforce an emotion by comforting a frightened dog, so soothing does not create fear. But if your dog is looking for an exit and you are holding it in place while telling it that it is fine, you are not helping. Give the dog distance first; comfort second.

When to see a vet first

A dog that becomes suddenly nervous, having previously been fine, goes to the vet before it comes to me. Pain is a common and badly under-diagnosed cause, and a dog that hurts when touched will not be trained out of avoiding hands.

What to do this week

  • Give the dog somewhere to go — a room or a crate behind a gate, with the door open, where nobody follows it. A dog that can leave is a dog that does not have to threaten
  • Put visitors behind a gate rather than the dog behind a door where you can. Shut away in another room, a worried dog spends the evening listening and getting worse
  • Brief people before they arrive, by message, so nobody has to be corrected in your hall
  • A lead on indoors for the first few minutes of a visit, held loose, so nothing depends on grabbing a collar
  • Feed it yourself, from your hand, while the visitor exists at a distance. The food should come from the safe person, not the frightening one
  • Keep visits short and end them early, while the dog is still coping. Stopping too late is what undoes a good evening

What to tell the visitor

Most people mean well and do exactly the wrong thing. Send this beforehand and it saves the whole conversation:

“Please ignore the dog completely — no talking to her, no eye contact, no reaching out, even if she comes over to sniff you. She will settle much faster. If she does come to you, just carry on talking to me.”

The dog approaching is not consent to be touched. A frightened dog often comes forward to gather information and then panics when a hand arrives — and a hand over the head from a stranger is, in dog terms, quite a rude thing to do.

Distance, and the food test

All of the useful work happens at a distance the dog can cope with, and there is a simple way to find it: a dog that will take food is a dog that is learning; a dog that will not is too close. Back off until it eats, and work there.

Outdoors is usually easier than indoors, because the dog can move away and nobody is standing in its house. It is often where the work starts.

What the work actually involves

  • A settle the dog genuinely likes, taught when nothing is happening, so it has somewhere to be when the door goes
  • A door routine rehearsed with staged arrivals, so the bell stops predicting chaos
  • Strangers at a distance who do nothing — people existing dully nearby, many times, is the whole engine of this work
  • Then closer, in small steps, on the dog’s terms, with the dog free to leave at every stage
  • Household rules, so a good six weeks is not undone by one guest who insisted on saying hello

Expect months rather than weeks. Fear work is among the most reliably fixable things I do and among the slowest, and progress is not a straight line — a fright can cost you a fortnight, which is why the management above matters as much as the training.

If it has gone beyond noise

If your dog has snapped, made contact, or backed a visitor into a corner, say so plainly when you ring. It does not change whether I take the case. It changes the plan: usually a muzzle taught properly and in advance, stricter management while we work, and a slower build. → behaviour consultation

What success looks like

A dog that hears the door, goes to its bed, and stays there while people come and go. Not a dog that greets everybody. Plenty of these dogs end up politely indifferent to visitors, which is a good outcome and a quiet house.

The goal is a dog that is comfortable in its own home while people come and go. Whether it ever loves visitors is up to the dog.
