Common problems · 03

Growled at, snapped at,
or bitten someone

If your dog has gone for a person, or you are afraid it is about to, this is the page for you. It is the call people put off longest, and the one I would most like to get early. I work with aggression. I trained for it, and I have been dealing with it in practice for many years.

First, you are not a bad owner

Most of the people who contact me about this are careful, loving owners who are shocked and a bit ashamed. The dog is lovely nine days out of ten. It sleeps on the bed. And then it snapped at a grandchild, or pinned the postman in the porch, or bit the hand that reached for its collar.

That does not make it a bad dog, and it does not make you a bad owner. It makes it a dog with a problem that needs someone to look at it properly. I will not judge you, and nothing you tell me will shock me.

Tonight, before anything else

Do not try to fix it yet. Just make it impossible for it to happen again while you get help.

  • Put a door, a gate or a crate between the dog and whoever it went for. Every time, not most times
  • Children and the dog are never together without an adult watching, and never at all around food, chews, beds or a sleeping dog
  • Do not punish it for growling. More on that below, but it matters enough to say twice
  • Do not test it to see whether it will do it again. Each repeat makes the next one more likely
  • Write down what happened while you remember: who, where, what the dog had, what happened in the minute before. That note is worth more to me than anything else you can send

A bite is the end of a conversation

Dogs very rarely bite out of nowhere. There is nearly always a run-up: turning the head away, licking the lips, going stiff, a hard stare, a growl, a snap at the air. Most of it is quiet and most of it gets missed. The bite is what happens when everything before it has not worked.

This is why a growl is good news. A dog that growls is still telling you how it feels and asking you to stop. Punish the growl and you do not get a happier dog. You get a dog that has learned to keep quiet until it bites. Those are the dogs people say bit “with no warning”, and they were usually taught to.

What is usually behind it

Not dominance, and not spite. In the dogs I see, it is nearly always one of a short list, and often two at once:

  • Fear. By a distance the most common. The dog wants something to go away and has found that this works
  • Guarding food, a toy, a bed, the sofa or a person. See guarding food and toys
  • Space. The doorway, the gate, the car, the kennel. Fine everywhere else
  • Being handled when it would rather not be: moved off the bed, held by the collar, nails, ears, the towel
  • Pain. A sore hip, an ear, a tooth. More cases than people expect

They can look identical from across the kitchen, and what helps one will make another worse. That is the whole reason I assess a dog before anyone starts training it. There is more on each of them on the behaviour consultation page.

See your vet first if it came from nowhere

A dog that was fine last month and is snapping this month goes to the vet before it comes to me. Pain, failing eyesight or hearing, thyroid trouble and some medicines can all change a dog’s temper. If there is a medical cause, no amount of training will touch it until that is treated.

What tends to make it worse

Telling it off or holding it down. You have joined the list of things the dog is worried about, at the moment it was already struggling.

Making it “face its fear”. Holding a frightened dog still while a stranger pets it does not teach it that strangers are safe. It teaches it that growling did not work.

Waiting to see if it grows out of it. This one rarely settles by itself. Each time the behaviour works, it is practised.

Advice from the internet. It is not always wrong. It is answering a question about a different dog.

What I do

It starts with a behaviour assessment, where the behaviour happens: your kitchen, your gate, your lane. I want the dog’s history, what has been tried, and what the dog has been getting out of it. You get a plain answer about what I think is going on, a written plan you can actually work, and a follow-up visit two to three weeks later.

From there most dogs need a block of one-to-one sessions. Some are better served by a stay with me, where I can work the dog every day. I will tell you which, and why, and what it will cost before you commit to anything.

The questions people are afraid to ask

Is my dog dangerous? Usually it is a frightened or confused dog in a situation it cannot cope with, and that can be changed. I will not guess from a description. I will tell you honestly once I have seen it.

Can it be fixed? Most dogs improve a great deal. Some will always need a few sensible rules around them, like a gate when visitors call. I guarantee the work, not the dog, and I will never promise you a result I cannot see my way to.

Will you tell me to have it put down? That is not where this starts, and it is very rarely where it ends. If I ever thought a dog could not live safely in a particular home, I would say so plainly and talk through every other option with you first.

Is it too late? No. But sooner is easier, cheaper and kinder than later.

If a child has been bitten or snapped at, please do not work through this from a webpage. Keep the dog and the children apart, with a gate, a crate or a closed door, and get in touch today.
