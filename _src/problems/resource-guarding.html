Common problems — 08

Growling over the bowl,
guarding the toys

A dog that stiffens when you walk past its dinner, or takes a toy under the table and warns you off it. This one frightens people more than almost anything else, and it is one of the most important to get right.

What it looks like

Eating faster as you approach. A hard stare over the bowl. A low growl when a child walks past. A dog that lies on a chew and goes still. Toys collected and defended in a corner. Sometimes it is only the food bowl, sometimes only one particular stolen item, sometimes the sofa or a person.

Please do not punish the growl

If you take one thing from this page, take this one.

A dog that growls is telling you it is uncomfortable and asking you to stop. That is a warning, and warnings are useful — they are the rungs on a ladder that runs from a hard look, to a growl, to bared teeth, to a bite. Punish the growl and you can absolutely make the growling stop. You have not made the dog comfortable. You have taught it that growling is dangerous, so next time it skips that rung.

That is where a dog that “bit with no warning” comes from. There was a warning. It got trained out.

Two different things that look the same

Possession. The dog has something good and does not intend to give it up. Ordinary, common, and generally very workable.

Genuine scarcity. A dog whose history taught it that food is not reliable — a rescue, a street dog, a puppy from a crowded litter. This one is not about manners at all, and it is handled quite differently.

The plans diverge early, so working out which one you have matters more than any technique.

Why it gets worse on its own

Every incident that ends with the dog keeping the item is a rehearsal. The strategy worked, so it gets stronger — and it tends to spread: first the bowl, then chews, then the spot by the fire, then a person on the sofa. Left alone this is one of the few problems that reliably generalises, which is why it is worth addressing sooner rather than waiting to see whether it settles.

Why the usual advice makes it worse

Taking the bowl away to show who is boss. This proves the dog's point. Hands near food now genuinely do predict losing the food.

Putting your hand in the food while it eats. Same problem, done more often.

Chasing the dog to retrieve a stolen sock. You have invented a wonderful game in which stolen items are the ticket to attention.

What actually works

Management first, immediately. Feed behind a closed door. Pick up the contested items. This is not the training — it is what stops the dog practising while the training happens.

Change what an approach predicts. The aim is a dog that looks up when you come near its bowl because your arrival has reliably meant something better arriving, not something being removed.

Trade, never take. A dog that has learned that giving something up produces a better thing has no reason to guard.

Teach a proper “leave” and a proper “give” somewhere calm and boring, long before either is needed over something valuable.

Sort the household out. Everyone has to do the same thing, and children need rules they can actually keep — which usually means the dog eats somewhere the children do not go.

When to ring rather than read

If a dog has already bitten or snapped over an item, or if there are children in the house, please do not work through this from a webpage. That is a case for someone standing in your kitchen watching what actually happens.

Guarding is not disloyalty and it is not the dog trying to run the house. It is a dog protecting something it values, using the only vocabulary it has.
