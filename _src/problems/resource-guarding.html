Common problems — 08

Growling over the bowl,
guarding the toys

A dog that stiffens when you walk past its dinner, or takes a toy under the table and warns you off it. This one frightens people more than almost anything else, and it is one of the most important to get right.

What it looks like

Eating faster as you approach. A hard stare over the bowl. A low growl when a child walks past. A dog that lies on a chew and goes still. Toys collected and defended in a corner. Sometimes it is only the food bowl, sometimes only one particular stolen item, sometimes the sofa or a person.

Please do not punish the growl

If you take one thing from this page, take this one.

A dog that growls is telling you it is uncomfortable and asking you to stop. That is a warning, and warnings are useful — they are the rungs on a ladder that runs from a hard look, to a growl, to bared teeth, to a bite. Punish the growl and you can absolutely make the growling stop. You have not made the dog comfortable. You have taught it that growling is dangerous, so next time it skips that rung.

That is where a dog that “bit with no warning” comes from. There was a warning. It got trained out.

Two different things that look the same

Possession. The dog has something good and does not intend to give it up. This is common, and it usually responds well to training.

Genuine scarcity. A dog whose history taught it that food is not reliable — a rescue, a street dog, a puppy from a crowded litter. This one is not about manners at all, and it is handled quite differently.

The plans diverge early, so working out which one you have matters more than any technique.

And a third thing that is not guarding at all

If the growling happens at the door, in the hall, at the gate or in the car rather than over an object, that is territory rather than possession — a place with edges the dog decided on, not a thing it owns. From the doorway the two look nearly identical, and they are handled differently, so it is worth separating early. See barking at every visitor.

They are not mutually exclusive either. Plenty of dogs do both.

Why it gets worse on its own

Every incident that ends with the dog keeping the item is a rehearsal. The strategy worked, so it gets stronger — and it tends to spread: first the bowl, then chews, then the spot by the fire, then a person on the sofa. Left alone this is one of the few problems that reliably generalises, which is why it is worth addressing sooner rather than waiting to see whether it settles.

Why the usual advice makes it worse

Taking the bowl away to show who is boss. This proves the dog's point. Hands near food now genuinely do predict losing the food.

Putting your hand in the food while it eats. Same problem, done more often.

Chasing the dog to retrieve a stolen sock. You have invented a wonderful game in which stolen items are the ticket to attention.

What to do this week

None of this is the training. It is how you stop the problem getting bigger while you arrange for somebody to look at it.

  • Feed the dog somewhere nobody has to walk past — a separate room, or behind a gate. A dog that eats in peace for a month is a dog that has not practised guarding for a month
  • Stop taking things off it. Every confiscation is evidence for the dog’s case
  • Pick the contested items up when the dog is out, not out of its mouth. If it is not on the floor it cannot be guarded
  • Trade, do not wrestle. If the dog has something it should not have, throw something better in the opposite direction and pick the item up while the dog is away from it
  • Nobody approaches a dog that has something. Not to pet it, not to look, not to take a photograph. Especially not children
  • Do not reach for the collar to move the dog away. Call it, or open a door, or throw food — hands near a guarded item is the exact situation you are trying to make safe

If the item is genuinely dangerous — a cooked bone, a medication packet — scatter a handful of food well away from the dog and collect the item while it hoovers. Grabbing is how people get bitten by dogs that have never bitten anybody.

Swapping, done properly

The exercise everybody half-knows, and it fails for one reason: the swap is done at the item, so the dog watches the hand arrive and take something. Done well, the food lands away from the item, the dog chooses to leave it, and the item is often given straight back.

Start with things the dog barely cares about, not the bone it would fight for. Work up slowly. What you are building is a dog that hears you coming and lifts its head in expectation rather than freezing — and that is a judgement about the dog’s face, not a number of repetitions, which is why it is worth having somebody watch the first few.

What the work actually involves

  • A list. What exactly gets guarded, from whom, at what distance, and what has already happened. Guarding is specific, and plans are built off the specifics
  • Management first, so nothing is rehearsed while we work
  • Changing the prediction: people approaching reliably means something good arriving, at a distance the dog is comfortable with, long before anybody is near the bowl
  • A drop and a recall built away from valued items, where they are easy, then brought closer
  • A settle the dog can take its chew to, so it has somewhere it genuinely will not be disturbed
  • Household rules everyone follows — and in a house with children, written on the fridge
  • Then new items and new people, because a dog that is fine with you over a bowl may not be fine with a visitor over a bone

When it is a person being guarded

A dog on the sofa that growls at a partner approaching, or at a child climbing up, is guarding a resource in the same way it would guard a bone. It tends to be read as devotion, which delays the phone call.

It usually responds well, and the early work is not confrontational: the dog comes off the furniture and the beds for a while, gets attention when it is asked for rather than when it is demanded, and learns that somebody approaching its person predicts something good. The reason to act early is that this is the version most likely to put a bite on a family member.

Children, specifically

Most bites to children in the home happen over food, a chew, or a dog that was asleep or cornered. Two rules, from today, in any house where a dog guards anything:

  • A child and a dog with food are never in the same room unsupervised. A gate is cheaper than a hospital visit
  • Children are taught to walk away and fetch an adult — never to take something off the dog, and never to be the one who deals with it

When it is the vet, not the trainer

A dog that has never guarded and suddenly starts is a medical question first. Pain makes anything worth defending. Failing sight makes an approaching hand a surprise. Some medications make a dog ravenous. Sudden change goes to your vet before it comes to me.

What success looks like

Not a dog you can take food from whenever you like — nobody needs that, and chasing it is how good progress gets undone. Success is a dog that is relaxed about people moving around it while it has something good, and a household that knows the handful of rules that keep it that way.

When to ring rather than read

If a dog has already bitten or snapped over an item, or if there are children in the house, please do not work through this from a webpage. That is a case for someone standing in your kitchen watching what actually happens.

Guarding is not disloyalty and it is not the dog trying to run the house. It is a dog protecting something it values, using the only vocabulary it has.
