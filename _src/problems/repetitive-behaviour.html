Spinning, pacing
and chasing shadows

Behaviour that goes round and round and gets the dog nowhere. It looks eccentric at first, and it is one of the few things on this list that becomes genuinely difficult if it is left.

What it looks like

Spinning on the spot. Chasing the tail. Pacing the same line of fence until there is a bare track worn into it. Snapping at flies that are not there. Chasing shadows, or the reflection off a watch face, or the bar of light that comes through the blinds at four o’clock. Licking one patch on a front leg until the hair is gone and the skin is not far behind.

What these have in common is that they repeat, and that they achieve nothing at all. The dog is not solving a problem or getting anywhere. That is what separates them from ordinary dog behaviour that simply happens to be annoying.

Where it comes from

Nearly always from a dog with more brain than it has anything to do with. And nearly always it began when something in the household changed.

So if a dog that was fine has stopped being fine, the questions worth asking yourself are these. Has the routine changed. Has somebody been away, or newly at home all day. Is the dog being walked less than it was. Has there been an illness or an operation in the family. Have you moved house. Has a baby arrived. Has the food or the medication changed. Has another dog come, or gone.

One case from the course that stuck with me: a nine-month-old dog began chasing shadows incessantly within a fortnight of its owner stopping the daily walk after a hip replacement. The dog was being let out into the garden instead, which is not the same thing at all. It was resolved not by treating the shadow-chasing but by teaching the dog to walk properly, so that the owner could safely take it out again. The shadow-chasing stopped almost immediately.

The pregnancy one, and the house-move one

Two versions of this come up often enough to be worth naming.

The first: a dog goes strange while somebody in the house is pregnant, and everyone decides the dog knows and has turned protective. It is nearly always the other thing. The walks got shorter, the games stopped, the household is thinking about something else, and a bored dog invented itself some work. The second is the same story in the weeks either side of a house move, with nobody at fault for it.

Why it matters is that putting the stimulation back tends to fix it, often within days — whereas “he is guarding me” leads people somewhere else entirely, and usually somewhere harsher.

Never a laser pointer

If you take one thing from this page, take this one. Laser pens are sold as exercise toys for dogs and they should not be.

A dog chasing a dot of light can never catch it. The sequence never finishes, and that unresolved frustration is close to a perfect recipe for building an obsessive pattern. Worse, it spreads: a dog played with this way starts in on watch faces, phone screens, the chrome on a car, the light off a glass of water, the strip that comes through the blinds. A garden hose does the same job. Once it is established it is among the hardest things described anywhere on this site to undo, and some of these dogs never entirely lose it.

See your vet first, particularly about licking

A dog licking or chewing at one spot — especially around the hips, the back end or a hind leg — goes to your vet before it comes to me. Very often that is pain rather than compulsion, and arthritis and hip trouble present in exactly this way.

Where the licking has already broken the skin it becomes self-sustaining, because healing skin itches, the dog licks, and the wound reopens. That one needs a vet and a trainer working at the same time. Neither of us resolves it alone.

An older dog is a different question

Confusion in an old dog is not this. A dog that gets stuck in front of a chair it could easily walk around, or stands in a corner and whines, or loses a route it has walked for ten years, may be in cognitive decline — and that is a veterinary conversation first. Worth saying that old dogs still learn perfectly well, and that keeping an ageing dog thinking is one of the better things you can do for it.

This is one of the few problems on the list that is far easier to prevent than to fix. If your dog has only just started, this is a good week to ring.
