The window that
closed too early

A two‑year‑old Great Dane who had never been properly socialised, and who had learned that a growl makes people go away. Several weeks of desensitisation — most of it done by her owner, not by me.

{{CASES_NAV}}

Stevie is a Great Dane, two years old, and she missed her socialisation almost entirely. Nothing dramatic happened to her. That is the point of the case: nothing had to.

By the time I met her, this is what she was doing. Anyone who caught her eye got a growl and bared teeth. Not a warning that built slowly — eye contact was the trigger, and the response came straight off the back of it. She could not be approached by a stranger at all, and she could not be walked safely on a lead anywhere with people in it.

She weighs 175 lb, and the growl that comes with her is deep enough to stop a grown adult mid‑stride. People read all of that as a dog about to do something. Almost none of it was.

A dog that growls at eye contact is usually asking for distance, not threatening to close it. The size changes how frightening it looks. It does not change what the dog is saying.

What was actually going on

There is a stretch early in a puppy’s life when new things are filed as normal more or less automatically. Strangers, hats, traffic, children, other dogs, hands reaching in — a puppy who meets all of that in the right window comes out the far side with a bank of evidence that says none of it matters. Stevie did not get that window. She arrived at adulthood with an empty bank.

So a stranger looking at her was not a neutral event she had two hundred examples of. It was new, and in dog terms a direct stare from something unfamiliar is pressure. Her answer to pressure was the only one she had.

And here is the part that had made it worse every week for two years: it worked. She growled, the person backed off, the pressure stopped. That is a behaviour being paid, reliably, several times a day, by strangers who had no idea they were training her. She was not getting more aggressive. She was getting better rehearsed.

The plan

Change what a stranger predicts, before asking her to do anything about strangers at all.

  1. 01

    Take eye contact off the table

    Nobody stares at her, nobody walks straight at her, nobody reaches over her head. The single thing that set her off was the easiest thing in the world to stop doing, and stopping it bought us a dog who could think.

  2. 02

    Work at a distance she can cope at

    Close enough to notice a person, far enough not to react to one. That distance is the whole dial. Everything after this is moving it in by a few feet at a time and being willing to move it back out again on a bad day.

  3. 03

    Pay the stranger, not the behaviour

    Food appears because a person appeared — not because Stevie did anything correct. That is deliberate. We are not training a sit near strangers, we are changing what a stranger means. The day she started taking food with people in view was the day we knew the stress had come down far enough to learn.

  4. 04

    Hand it to the owner

    Short, frequent, boring repetitions on ordinary walks, every day, done properly. The sessions set the homework up. The homework is what actually moved her.

Why the owner’s half matters more than mine

Worth being blunt about this, because it is the part clients underestimate. Private one‑to‑one sessions were a small fraction of the hours that changed this dog. Two years of rehearsal does not get outvoted in an hour a week.

Desensitisation is a repetitions‑over‑time business. It happens on the school run and the evening walk, in the gap between sessions, done by the person holding the lead. Stevie’s owner did that work — consistently, and at the boring end of it, which is the hard part. A trainer who tells you otherwise is selling you their own hours.

How long it took

Several weeks, and it is not finished. I am not going to round that up into a number that sounds better. A fear history built over two years is not unpicked on a schedule, and anybody quoting you one for this kind of case is guessing.

Where she is now

She is not growling at people. She is taking treats. She will allow herself to be patted. And she can be walked past people without reacting to them, which is the one her owner actually wanted, because it is the difference between a dog you can take out and a dog you cannot.

Every one of those is the same underlying change showing up in a different place: strangers stopped predicting pressure.

Three clips. This is the first: Stevie with me, on an empty stretch of road, nothing else going on.

Watch the lead rather than the dog. It hangs. A 175 lb dog who has decided to walk with you does not need to be held anywhere, and that slack is the thing actually being trained. The walk is only where it shows up.

This is also the easy version, and it has to be solid before either of the next two is worth attempting.

That is not me on the end of the lead, and that is the point of showing it. A dog who will only hold it together for the person who trained her has not finished learning anything — she has just learned that one person is safe. The behaviour has to travel to other hands, or it is not trained, it is borrowed.

And this is the one that counts. There are four people on the road ahead of her, walking towards her. She is looking at them. And she keeps walking.

A few weeks earlier, any one of those people glancing at her would have got a growl.

What this case does not claim

Stevie is not a finished dog and we have some distance to go. What has changed is her default. The old one is still in there, and under the wrong combination — cornered, startled, a stranger who reaches in over the top of her — it is still the answer she reaches for first. That is not a failure of the work. It is what a fear history is.

Her owner still manages her rather than assuming her. That is the correct way to hold a dog like this, and it does not stop being correct because the growling stopped.

And the obvious one, which is the reason this case is on the site at all: none of this would have been necessary. Socialisation at the formative stages should never be underestimated. It is a handful of weeks of ordinary, unglamorous exposure while a puppy is young, and the alternative is what you have just read — several weeks of careful work, ongoing, to get back to a starting line she should never have been behind.
