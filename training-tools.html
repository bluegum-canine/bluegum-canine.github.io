Training tools

Nobody sells you
the instructions

You can buy a prong collar or an electronic collar in a shop or online, this afternoon, with no questions asked and nothing in the box telling you how to use it. That is where most of the harm gets done — not by the tool, but by a decent owner using it the way it looks like it should be used.

About me The qualification Training tools

Sold everywhere, taught nowhere

You can have one delivered tomorrow. What you cannot easily find is a single person who will sit down and show you how to use it. So people do the only thing left and learn off the internet, where the subject has been reduced to two camps shouting past each other: one saying the things are cruel and should be banned outright, the other posting thirty-second clips of a transformed dog with no account of what came before or after. Neither is the slightest use to someone standing in their kitchen holding one they have already bought.

The myths do real damage in both directions. That the tools work by hurting the dog — they do not, when they are fitted and set correctly, and a level found properly is one most people would not feel through their own fingertips. That they are a shortcut for lazy trainers — used properly they take longer to introduce than most owners expect, not less. And the one going the other way, which is just as wrong: that buying one solves the problem. It solves nothing on its own.

The tool is not the question. The use of it is. Almost every bad outcome I have seen or read about comes down to a decent owner with no instruction, not to the equipment itself — and the answer to that is teaching, not pretending the thing does not exist.

Part of why nobody teaches it is that a good many trainers who do know how have learned to keep quiet. Saying so publicly costs work and buys an argument, so the subject gets dodged, and the person who most needs showing is left with a shop assistant and a video. I would rather say where I stand and be judged on it.

What usually goes wrong

Almost always the same thing: the tool is used to teach. The dog does not yet know what is being asked, and pressure arrives to explain it. That is giving the test before the lesson, and the dog's conclusion is rarely the one intended.

Tools do not teach. They reinforce something already taught by other means. Get that order wrong and you produce a dog that is confused and worried rather than one that understands, and the owner concludes the tool "doesn't work on him" — or worse, that more of it is needed.

The second thing that goes wrong

Fit and level. A prong collar sitting low on the neck does something quite different from one fitted properly. An electronic collar set by guesswork, rather than found by working up from a level the dog barely perceives, is a different instrument altogether.

On the course, working levels were found by watching for an ear twitch or a blink — typically far lower than people assume, and different for every dog. Most people who own one have never been shown that.

When it is the kinder answer

Used properly and in the right case, these are frequently the fastest and the gentlest route to a dog that can be given its freedom back — which is the whole point of the exercise. Two cases make that obvious.

An owner who physically cannot hold the dog. If you have a shoulder that gives out, a balance problem, or you are simply not heavy enough to anchor what you have got, then a dog that pulls is not a training inconvenience. It is the reason you stop walking it, and a dog that stops being walked loses far more than it would ever lose to a well-fitted collar. Managing that dog safely is not a failure of patience.

A deaf dog. A dog that cannot hear you cannot be recalled by voice, and hand signals only work while it is looking at you — which is precisely not the moment you need it. A collar that can deliver a gentle vibration gives that dog a way of being told something from a distance, and it is the difference between a life on a lead and a life off one.

I am not claiming either case is typical. Most of what I do involves none of this. But a blanket objection to the tool has to be set against the dog that does not get walked and the deaf dog that never gets off a lead, and I have not heard a good answer to either.

What I can do about it

If you already own one and want to use it properly, I will show you: fit, timing, level, and the order things have to be taught in for the tool to be fair.

If you would rather not use one, that is an entirely reasonable position and most of what I do does not involve them at all. Say so at the assessment and it is simply not part of the plan.

If you own one and it is making things worse, that is worth a conversation sooner rather than later. It is a common enough reason for a call and there is no lecture waiting at the other end.

Where I stand

Plainly, so you
can decide

I use a balanced approach. Reward and consequence both have a place and the judgement is in knowing which the situation calls for. I am not going to pretend otherwise, because you would find out eventually and it is better said now.

Correction is reserved for behaviours with real safety consequences. Nuisance habits are better solved by changing the setup than by correcting the dog. A tool is never the first answer and frequently not the answer at all.

I do not sell tools, and I have no interest in talking anyone into one. What I object to is a shop selling a piece of equipment to someone who has been given no idea how to use it, and a trade that would rather argue about whether it should exist than teach the person who already owns it.

What I am actually qualified to say

Tools are a module of the Master Trainer programme in their own right — forty hours, split evenly between theory and work with dogs, across the gentle leader, harness, martingale, prong collar and remote collar. Both halves were examined, the written paper and the practical, under exam conditions. Explaining a tool and using one on a live dog are different skills, and they were tested as different skills.

What has happened since counts for more. I have gone on using them through the months that followed, on a great many dogs, where they were the right answer and not where they were not — and that is where the judgement actually gets built. A qualification tells you a person was taught something. It does not tell you they have done it often enough to be any good at it.

I have also spent the last three months helping to teach it, rather than only doing it. Three evenings a week I assist in group obedience classes for paying owners, working with whatever has come through the door on whatever they already own. That is a different test again. Fitting and timing a tool yourself is one thing; putting it into somebody else’s hands, in a hall, on a Tuesday, and having them get it right is the thing you would actually be hiring me for.

Alongside the programme I hold the International Association of Canine Professionals’ Prong Collar Knowledge Assessment, passed in September 2026 and marked independently of the course. I am a full member of the IACP and bound by its code of conduct.
