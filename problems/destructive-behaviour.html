Common problems › Destructive at home

Common problems — 05

Destructive
at home

Chewing, digging and damage while you are out. Expensive, wearing, and almost never what the owner thinks it is.

What it looks like

Skirting boards, table legs, shoes, the corner of a sofa. Holes in the lawn or in the bed. Post shredded on the mat. Wallpaper stripped to chest height. And, very often, the owner coming home to a dog with what looks unmistakably like a guilty face.

The guilty look

Worth dealing with first, because it drives so much bad handling. That expression is appeasement, not guilt. It is a response to your posture and tone in the moment — and it appears just as reliably in dogs that did nothing at all, if the owner comes in expecting a mess. Your dog is not connecting your face now with a chewed chair leg two hours ago.

Which is why punishing on discovery does nothing except teach the dog that your homecoming is unpredictable and unpleasant. In an anxious dog it makes the underlying problem measurably worse.

Why it happens — four different reasons

The treatment is completely different in each case, so the diagnosis matters.

Teething and normal puppy exploration. Under about seven months, chewing is developmental. It needs management and appropriate outlets, not correction.

Boredom and under-stimulation. A dog with nothing to do and eight hours to do it in will find work. Common in young, clever, working-bred dogs — which in this part of the country means a great many collies.

Anxiety. The most commonly missed. Destruction that is focused on exit points — door frames, window sills, the carpet in front of the door — is a different animal from a chewed cushion, and points at panic rather than mischief. Read this alongside the velcro dog.

It has been accidentally rewarded. Digging is self-rewarding. So is shredding. And some dogs have learned that picking up a shoe reliably produces a chase around the kitchen, which is the best game in the house.

Why the usual fixes stall

More walking. Helps a genuinely under-exercised dog and does nothing for an anxious one. It is the first thing everybody tries and the reason so many people conclude nothing works.

Leaving the television on. Harmless, rarely sufficient.

A cupboard full of chew toys. Useful only if the dog has been taught to use them, and actively counter-productive if the toy box contains an old shoe.

Crating without preparation. A crate is an excellent tool and a terrible punishment. Shut an anxious dog in a box it has not been taught to like and you will get a worse problem and possibly an injured dog.

What actually works

Find out which of the four it is. A camera for one session usually settles it. A dog that destroys in the first twenty minutes and then sleeps is telling you something quite different from one that works at the door for two hours.

Manage while you train. Every unsupervised repetition makes the habit stronger, so the dog does not get the opportunity while the work is going on. Management is not cheating; it is what makes the training possible.

Give the behaviour somewhere legitimate to go. Dogs need to chew, dig and use their noses. A dog with a proper outlet and a legal place to do it is far easier than a dog being asked to stop entirely.

Work the mind, not just the legs. Scent work, food-searching, and structured training tire a clever dog far more effectively than another mile of road.

Treat anxiety as anxiety. If that is what it is, no amount of enrichment will touch it and the plan is an entirely different one.

Almost none of this is spite. Nearly all of it is a dog solving a problem the only way it has available.
