Common problems — 04

The velcro dog

Follows you room to room and cannot settle alone. It is flattering for about a fortnight and then it becomes the reason you cannot go to the shop.

What it looks like

A dog at your heel from the kettle to the bathroom door. A dog that stands up the moment you do. Whining or pacing when you leave the room, never mind the house. An inability to lie down and switch off while anything at all is happening.

Two different problems that look identical

This matters more than anything else on the page, because the two need opposite handling.

Over-attachment and no off-switch. The dog has simply never learned to be alone or to rest. It follows because following has always been available and nothing has ever taught it otherwise. It may whine when you leave, but it settles reasonably soon afterwards.

Separation anxiety. A genuine panic response. The dog does not merely dislike being alone, it cannot cope with it — sustained howling, drooling, destruction focused on doors and windows, toileting indoors in a house-trained dog, occasionally self-injury. This is a welfare problem, not a manners problem, and it needs a proper behaviour plan and sometimes veterinary involvement.

The way to tell them apart is a camera. Film the dog while you are out, for a full hour or two. Most owners are relieved by what they see; some are not, and either way it changes the plan entirely. Guessing wastes months.

Why it happens

Some of it is us. A dog that has never once been asked to be elsewhere has no reason to invent the idea. Many velcro dogs were acquired at a time when someone was home constantly, and the routine that formed then became the only routine the dog knows.

Some of it is rest. A surprising number of dogs described as clingy or hyperactive are chronically under-rested rather than under-exercised. Dogs need a great deal of sleep, and a dog living in a busy household with no quiet place is on duty all day.

And some of it is that following gets paid. The dog follows, something happens — attention, a door held open, a dropped crumb. It works, so it continues.

Why the usual fixes stall

More exercise. The most common advice and often exactly wrong. A tired dog is not necessarily a settled dog, and an arousal-heavy routine of ball-throwing can produce a fitter dog that is harder to switch off, not easier.

Getting a second dog for company. This rarely fixes it and frequently doubles the problem. If the attachment is to you, a second dog does not substitute; and you now have two dogs, one of whom may learn the behaviour from the other.

Ignoring the dog completely. Blunt and unkind, and it does nothing to teach the dog what to do instead.

What actually works

Teach settling as a behaviour. Not as an absence of activity but as a thing the dog is actually taught, in the same four steps as anything else: a place, a duration, then distraction, then maintenance. Most velcro dogs have never been taught to lie down and do nothing.

Build distance in inches. Behind a baby gate while you are visible. Then out of sight for ten seconds. Then thirty. The increments are much smaller than people expect, and going too fast is what causes the setbacks.

Make departures boring. No emotional farewell, no reunion party. Pick up your keys and sit back down, twenty times, until keys stop meaning anything.

Protect sleep. A quiet, defensible place that nobody disturbs, and enough hours in it.

Break the pattern of paid following. Kindly and consistently, so that trailing you to the kitchen simply stops producing anything of interest.

The aim is a dog that chooses to be near you, not one that cannot bear to be anywhere else.
