Common problems — 06

Livestock
worrying

The most serious problem a dog can have in the west of Ireland. It is the one behaviour on this list that can get your dog shot, lawfully, in front of you.

What counts

Worrying is not only killing or injuring. Chasing sheep is worrying. So is being loose among them in a way that causes them distress — and in-lamb ewes do not need to be touched to lose lambs. A farmer whose flock has been run does not have to show you a wound to have suffered a real loss.

It is also not confined to any particular type of dog. The friendly family Labrador that has never shown a moment’s aggression will chase sheep enthusiastically, because chasing is not aggression — it is predatory behaviour, and it is enormously self-rewarding.

Where you stand legally

Under the Control of Dogs Act 1986, a dog owner is liable for damage their dog does when attacking livestock, and the injured party does not have to prove that the owner was careless. The Act also provides a defence for a person who kills or injures a dog to protect livestock, subject to conditions including reporting it to the Gardaí promptly. Dogs are required to be under effectual control.

In plain terms: if your dog chases stock, the loss is likely to be yours to pay for, and a farmer who shoots it may well be within their rights. Sheep worrying is also one of the fastest routes to a dog being destroyed by order.

This is a plain-English summary and not legal advice. If you are facing a claim or a prosecution, talk to a solicitor.

Why it happens

Chasing sits on the ordinary predatory sequence — orient, stalk, chase, grab. Almost every dog has some of it, and generations of selective breeding have amplified particular parts in particular breeds. The chase itself produces a flood of arousal that is intensely rewarding, which means the behaviour reinforces itself without anyone having to do anything.

That is why it escalates so fast. A dog that has had one good run at a flock is a substantially more dangerous dog the following week. There are few behaviours where early intervention matters more.

Why the usual fixes stall

“He only did it once.” Once is how it starts, and once is enough to have taught the dog that it is worth doing.

Calling louder. A dog in full chase is in a state where a normal recall simply does not reach. If your recall has not been proofed against serious competition, it will not hold here — see recall.

Treats near sheep. Food does not compete with a predatory sequence in full flow. It has a role early and at distance, but not as the plan.

Hoping the farm dog will teach him. It will not.

What actually works

This is one of the few problems where I will say plainly that management comes first and stays first, permanently.

Absolute containment while you train. Secure fencing, a long line anywhere near stock, and no off-lead access to land with livestock on it. Not as a punishment — as the thing that keeps your dog alive long enough to be trained.

A recall and a stop that are genuinely proofed. Not a garden recall. A recall built through distance, distraction and real temptation, plus an emergency stop, so that you have something that works at range and under arousal.

Give the chase somewhere legal to go. The drive does not disappear because you disapprove of it. Structured play that lets the dog chase something you control gives that arousal a legitimate outlet, and it should be established before anyone tries to shut the illegal one down.

Then, and only then, controlled work near stock. At distance, on a line, with the landowner’s knowledge and permission, building gradually. This is where a balanced approach earns its keep: this is exactly the kind of absolute no-go behaviour, with real safety consequences, where correction is appropriate — and it is applied with judgement, on a dog that already knows what the alternative is.

An honest word

Some dogs with a serious, established chase history end up as dogs that are managed for life rather than cured — safe, happy, and never off a line near stock. I would far rather tell you that at the start than sell you a programme and a promise I cannot keep.

If your dog has chased stock even once, ring somebody this week. This is not a problem that waits well.
