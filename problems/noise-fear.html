Common problems › Thunder and fireworks

Common problems — 10

Frightened of thunder
and fireworks

A dog shaking behind the sofa, panting, pacing, trying to get under something. For a lot of households this is two bad weeks a year, and for some it is every time the sky darkens.

What it looks like

Trembling, drooling, pacing, hiding in a bathroom or a wardrobe. Refusing food. Trying to dig through a floor. Some dogs bolt, which is how noise fear turns into a lost dog and the reason it is worth taking seriously rather than waiting out.

Many owners also notice the dog knows before they do — unsettled an hour before a storm arrives, because the pressure change and the light have become part of the warning.

You cannot spoil a frightened dog by comforting it

This is worth saying plainly, because a lot of people have been told the opposite and then felt guilty for sitting with their dog.

You cannot reinforce an emotion. Fear is not a behaviour the dog is choosing in order to earn something; it is how the dog feels. If your dog wants to be near you during a storm, let it. Comfort does not create cowardice.

What you can accidentally reinforce is a specific behaviour attached to the fear — and that is a different and much narrower thing. It is worth having someone look at the actual pattern rather than guessing which you have.

Be specific about the trigger

Fears are usually far narrower than the label suggests. “Afraid of thunder” often turns out to be the low rumble rather than the crack, or the flash rather than the sound, or the rain on a particular roof. A dog described as frightened of fireworks may be entirely untroubled by the bangs and undone by the whistle.

Getting that right is most of the plan, because you cannot work on a trigger you have not identified.

What does not work

Making the dog face it. A frightened animal that cannot escape does not learn that the noise is harmless; it learns that it is trapped as well as frightened.

Before the season, not on the night

Halloween and New Year are the two predictable ones here, and both are worse for everyone who leaves it until the week before.

  • Build the den now. An interior room with the fewest windows, curtains closed, a radio or a fan running, bedding the dog already likes. Leave it open and use it on ordinary days, so it is a familiar place rather than an emergency one
  • Never shut a panicking dog in. A dog that wants out of a crate will hurt itself trying, and a door it can open is worth more than any bedding
  • Check the microchip details and the collar tag, and walk the boundary of the garden. More dogs go missing around Halloween than at any other time, and a frightened dog will get through a gap it has never noticed before
  • Walk early. Exercise and toileting done in daylight, well before anything starts
  • Feed earlier than usual. A dog that is already worried by nine o’clock will not eat its dinner at ten

Talk to your vet early

For a dog that genuinely panics, there is a conversation worth having with your vet weeks beforehand rather than on the day. There are medicines intended for exactly this, and there are older sedatives that leave a dog unable to move while still perfectly frightened. Your vet will know the difference. Going in early also means there is time to try a dose on a quiet evening rather than discovering how your dog reacts on the worst night of the year.

That is a veterinary decision, not a training one — but a dog that is not in a state of panic is a dog that can learn something, so the two work together rather than against each other.

What the work actually involves

  • Working out which part of it the dog fears — the bang, the whistle, the flash, the vibration, the pressure change
  • Starting out of season, months before it matters, when nothing frightening is due to happen
  • Sound at a level the dog barely notices, paired with something it genuinely loves, then raised by very small amounts
  • Accepting the limits of recordings. Storms are not only sound — there is vibration, light, air pressure and rain — and a dog can be word-perfect with a recording and undone by the real thing. Storm work usually needs real weather, caught as it comes
  • A settled routine on the night, so the dog has a plan rather than a scramble

What success looks like

Not a dog that sleeps through fireworks. A dog that takes itself to its den, settles, and eats something. That is a realistic target, and for most dogs it is reachable — while a dog left to cope alone usually gets worse each year, because every bad night is another rehearsal.

Noise fear responds well to slow, unglamorous work done months before it is needed — and very badly to anything attempted on the night.
