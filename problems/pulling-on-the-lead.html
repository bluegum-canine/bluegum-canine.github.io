Common problems › Pulling on the lead

Common problems — 01

Pulling on
the lead

A walk that is a battle rather than a pleasure. For most owners this is the problem that quietly shrinks the dog’s life, because a dog that is hard work to walk gets walked less.

What it looks like

Leaning into the collar the whole way. Arms pulled out of their sockets. Choosing quiet routes and odd hours to avoid meeting anyone. Handing the lead to whoever in the house is strongest — and often a shoulder or a wrist that has started to complain about it.

Why it happens

Pulling is not defiance and it is not dominance. It is the single most heavily rehearsed behaviour in most dogs’ lives, and it works. The dog pulls, the dog moves forward, the world arrives faster. Every walk since puppyhood has paid that behaviour off, several hundred times a walk, and no training session competes with that volume of practice.

There is a mechanical part too. Pressure on the front of a dog’s chest triggers an oppositional reflex — push against a dog and it pushes back. A tight lead therefore creates some of the very pulling it is trying to stop, which is why the walk tends to get worse the harder you hold on.

Why the usual fixes stall

Most people arrive having already bought equipment. Harnesses are the usual one, and a standard back-clip harness is close to the perfect pulling tool — it is what you would put on a dog if you actively wanted it to pull a load comfortably. It makes the walk more bearable for a while by spreading the pressure, and in doing so it lets the dog rehearse pulling for longer without discomfort.

The other common approach is treats at heel. That often produces a lovely heel in the driveway and nothing at all at the gate, because the behaviour was taught and then never proofed. It also runs straight into the second principle: rewarded on every step, it is learned quickly and lost just as quickly.

What actually works

Three things together, in order.

First, stop paying for it. Pulling has to stop producing forward movement — every single time, not most times. Dogs cope well with always and with never, and very badly with sometimes. This is the part that needs everyone in the house to agree, and it is usually where the real work is.

Second, teach the alternative properly. The dog needs to know what you do want, in a quiet place, before anyone expects it on a road. This step should take the longest and is nearly always rushed.

Third, proof it. Distance, duration and distraction, built up deliberately — the gate, the road, another dog across the way, a farm. A loose lead that only exists in the garden has been practised, not proofed.

Equipment is chosen to suit the dog and the handler, and I will explain what I am recommending and why. For most family dogs the equipment matters far less than the consistency.

How long it takes

Owners usually see the walk change within the first two or three sessions, because the biggest single change is in what the handler does. Making it durable — proofed and maintained — takes a few weeks of short, frequent, deliberate walks. Short and frequent beats long and occasional every time.

A dog that walks nicely gets taken places. That is freedom through obedience, in its most everyday form.
