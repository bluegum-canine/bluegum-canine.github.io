Common problems — 09

Barking at
every visitor

The doorbell goes and the house erupts. Five minutes of noise before anyone can hear themselves speak, and a dog that has to be shut in the kitchen every time somebody calls.

What it looks like

Barking at the bell, at a knock, at a car on the gravel, at the postman at the same time every morning. Some dogs settle once the visitor is in and sitting down. Some do not settle at all. Some are perfectly friendly the whole time and simply extremely loud about it.

Why it is so hard to shift

Because from the dog's side, it works every single time.

The postman arrives. The dog barks. The postman leaves. From the dog's point of view that is a job well done, repeated daily, for years. This is the most reliably reinforced behaviour in most households and nobody ever intended to train it.

Add to that the fact that barking at an arrival is genuinely a dog-shaped thing to do. We are not correcting a fault so much as renegotiating a habit the dog considers useful.

Work out which one you have

Alerting. Something happened and the dog is announcing it. Usually stops once the situation is resolved and the visitor is in.

Excitement. Visitors are wonderful and the dog cannot contain it. Often accompanied by jumping and a great deal of movement.

Fear. The dog does not want the visitor to come in and is saying so. This one gets worse if it is punished, and it is more common than owners expect — a dog can be delighted to see you and genuinely worried about a stranger in the hall.

The three look similar in the first ten seconds and need different plans, which is why the first thing I do is watch an arrival.

And then there is territory

Some of these dogs are not alerting, not excited and not frightened. They are defending a place.

Guarding a thing and guarding a place are two different problems. A bed, a bone or a spot on the sofa is a resource — that is guarding food and toys. A hall, a yard, a gateway or the car is territory, and the part that catches people out is that the dog drew the boundary, not you. Your dog has not read the deeds. A dog walked the same way every morning will often come to treat the whole route as its own, which is why some dogs are worse two hundred yards down the road than they are at their own front door.

Announcing a stranger is not in itself a fault. Most dogs do it and most households actually want it. It becomes a problem when it stops being noise and posturing and turns into teeth — the postman, the meter reader, the visitor who put a hand out in the hall.

Two things worth knowing before you ring anybody. The great majority of these dogs are under-occupied, so an honest look at what the dog does with the rest of its day is part of the plan rather than an afterthought. And having the dog neutered will not fix this. It is suggested constantly, and for this particular problem there is nothing behind it at all.

What to do this week

  • Take the doorbell out of the equation. Disconnect it, or put a note asking people to ring your phone instead. You are not living like that forever — you are stopping the rehearsal while you retrain it
  • Put the dog behind a gate before you open the door, every time, with something good to chew. The dog is not being punished; it is being kept out of a situation it always loses
  • Have something to give it at the door, ready in a pot by the hall, so the arrival predicts food rather than a confrontation
  • Deal with the window. A dog that patrols a front window is on duty all day. Frosted film, a closed curtain or a different room while you are out breaks a habit that is otherwise practised every hour
  • Look at the rest of the day. A dog with too little to do makes the arrival of the postman the event of the morning

The postman is the hardest part

Because he is the one trigger that pays off every single day, on schedule, whatever anybody does. The dog barks, the man leaves, the dog is right again.

The way out is to change what the arrival means before it means anything else. If you know the round is around eleven, be ready at quarter to: dog away from the window, food in hand, and every delivery followed by something good happening away from the door. Some owners go further and ask the postman to drop a piece of food through the letterbox, which turns the daily rehearsal on its head.

Post that arrives when nobody is home is still being rehearsed. That is what the closed curtain is for.

What the work actually involves

  • A place the dog goes to when the door goes, taught when nothing is happening, until it is a habit rather than an instruction
  • Staged arrivals — dozens of them, boring ones, with a friend knocking and leaving and nothing else happening — so the sound stops predicting an event
  • Deciding what you actually want. Most households want two barks and then quiet, not silence. Two barks is an easier thing to train than none, and it is honest about what dogs are for
  • A way to end it: the dog is thanked, called away, and given something to do
  • Then real visitors, briefed in advance, in the same pattern

When barking has turned into teeth

A dog that has nipped a visitor, or that a delivery driver is frightened of, is no longer a barking problem. That is a behaviour case, and it is worth remembering that a dog that bites somebody lawfully on your property is your liability. → behaviour consultation

Why shouting does not help

To a dog that is already barking, a person raising their voice is a person joining in. Whatever you meant, what arrived was noise at the same moment as the trigger — and in an anxious dog, that makes the arrival more alarming rather than less.

The aim is not a silent dog. It is a dog that tells you somebody is here and then lets you deal with it.
