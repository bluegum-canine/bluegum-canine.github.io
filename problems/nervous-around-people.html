Common problems › Nervous around people

Common problems — 03

Nervous
around people

Shrinking, barking or hiding when visitors call. This one upsets owners more than any other on the list, because it is hard to watch and easy to take personally.

What it looks like

Backing away from strangers. Barking furiously at the door and then retreating behind the sofa. Tucked tail, flattened ears, licking lips, turning away, refusing food that would normally be taken. Sometimes it presents as the opposite of nervousness — a dog that rushes forward barking — and the two are frequently the same thing wearing different clothes.

Why it happens

Fear is not a training failure and it is not a character flaw. It comes from some mixture of genetics, what the dog did or did not meet between roughly three and sixteen weeks, and anything unpleasant that has happened since. Rescue dogs often arrive with a history nobody can reconstruct, and it is not always necessary to know it.

What keeps it going is more useful than what started it. Barking at a stranger works — the stranger goes away, or the dog retreats and the frightening thing stops. That is a behaviour being paid off, every time, and it becomes a reliable strategy the dog reaches for first.

Why the usual fixes stall

“Let them meet lots of people, they’ll get used to it.” Flooding an already-frightened dog with the thing it fears usually confirms the fear rather than curing it. Socialisation means controlled, positive exposure at a distance the dog can cope with — not simply more contact.

Asking visitors to offer a treat. This puts food directly next to the frightening thing and pulls a conflicted dog closer than it wanted to go. Dogs bite in exactly that situation, having taken the food first.

Telling the dog off for growling. The most damaging thing on this page. A growl is information, and a dog that has been punished for growling has not become less frightened — it has learned to skip the warning. That is how a dog becomes one that “bit with no warning”.

Reassurance is more complicated than either camp claims. You cannot reinforce an emotion by comforting a frightened dog, so soothing does not create fear. But if your dog is looking for an exit and you are holding it in place while telling it that it is fine, you are not helping. Give the dog distance first; comfort second.

What actually works

Distance is the tool. Every fearful dog has a range at which it can notice something without panicking. All the useful work happens at that distance, and it is nearly always further away than people expect.

Change the association before asking for behaviour. The stranger predicts something good, at a distance the dog can handle, repeatedly, before anyone tries to close the gap.

Give the dog somewhere to go. A bed or crate that visitors never approach, and an absolute household rule that nobody follows the dog there. A guaranteed exit lowers the stakes enormously, and dogs that know they can leave usually choose to stay.

Visitors ignore the dog. No eye contact, no reaching, no calling it over. Let the dog make the approach, and let it be the one that ends it.

Predictability. Fearful dogs are steadied more by routine than by anything else, because a world that behaves consistently is a world that requires less vigilance.

When to see a vet first

A dog that becomes suddenly nervous, having previously been fine, goes to the vet before it comes to me. Pain is a common and badly under-diagnosed cause, and a dog that hurts when touched will not be trained out of avoiding hands.

The goal is not a dog that loves everybody. It is a dog that is comfortable in its own home while people come and go.
