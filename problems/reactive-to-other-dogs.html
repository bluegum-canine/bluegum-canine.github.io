Common problems — 07

Barking and lunging
at other dogs

The single most common reason people ring me. A walk that has to be planned around who else might be out, and a dog that turns into something unrecognisable the moment it sees another dog.

What it looks like

Spotting the other dog first, every time. The stiffening, then the lunge, then the noise. Owners describe being dragged, or standing in a gateway waiting for someone to pass, or driving somewhere else entirely to walk at six in the morning. Very often the dog is completely fine with dogs it already knows, which makes the whole thing feel inexplicable.

“Reactive” is not a diagnosis

It is a description of the noise, and it tells us nothing about the cause. A dog that barks and lunges at other dogs may be doing it for several quite different reasons, and they need different plans:

Fear. By far the most common. The dog wants distance and has learned that making a scene creates it. Every time the other dog passes by, the strategy worked.

Frustration. The dog desperately wants to get to the other dog and cannot, because there is a lead in the way. This one is often mistaken for aggression and handled as though it were, which makes it considerably worse.

Genuine aggression toward other dogs, which is real but less common than the label suggests, and which needs a much more careful plan.

Telling these apart is most of the work, and it is the reason I want to see the dog rather than take a description over the phone. Getting it wrong does not simply waste time — it usually makes things worse.

How the lead makes it worse

This is the part that surprises people most, so it is worth being precise about.

When a dog barks at something it is worried about and the handler checks the lead, the intention is a correction. What frequently happens is the opposite. The dog is already anxious; the pressure arrives at the same moment as the trigger, and the whole situation becomes more alarming rather than less. So the dog starts reacting earlier — at twenty metres, then thirty, then forty — getting in first, before the unpleasant thing can happen.

Owners often describe this as the dog “getting worse for no reason”. There is a reason. The pattern was built accidentally, one walk at a time.

Why the usual advice stalls

Flooding. Taking a worried dog to a busy park so it can “get used to it”. A dog that cannot escape does not habituate; it rehearses panic.

Meeting other dogs head-on to say hello. Two dogs on tight leads, face to face, is a genuinely rude greeting in dog terms and an odd thing to ask of a dog that is already unsure.

Punishing the noise. The bark is a warning. Suppress the warning and the fear underneath it is untouched — you have simply removed the part that let you see it coming.

A dog that barks at other dogs is not a bad dog, and it is very rarely a badly-raised one. It is a dog whose strategy has been working.
