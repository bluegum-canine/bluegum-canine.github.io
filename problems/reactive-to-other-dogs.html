Common problems — 07

Barking and lunging
at other dogs

The single most common reason people ring me. A walk that has to be planned around who else might be out, and a dog that turns into something unrecognisable the moment it sees another dog.

What it looks like

Spotting the other dog first, every time. The stiffening, then the lunge, then the noise. Owners describe being dragged, or standing in a gateway waiting for someone to pass, or driving somewhere else entirely to walk at six in the morning. Very often the dog is completely fine with dogs it already knows, which makes the whole thing feel inexplicable.

“Reactive” is not a diagnosis

It is a description of the noise, and it tells us nothing about the cause. A dog that barks and lunges at other dogs may be doing it for several quite different reasons, and they need different plans:

Fear. By far the most common. The dog wants distance and has learned that making a scene creates it. Every time the other dog passes by, the strategy worked.

Frustration. The dog desperately wants to get to the other dog and cannot, because there is a lead in the way. This one is often mistaken for aggression and handled as though it were, which makes it considerably worse.

Genuine aggression toward other dogs, which is real but less common than the label suggests, and which needs a much more careful plan.

Telling these apart is most of the work, and it is the reason I want to see the dog rather than take a description over the phone. Getting it wrong does not simply waste time — it usually makes things worse.

How the lead makes it worse

This is the part that surprises people most, so it is worth being precise about.

When a dog barks at something it is worried about and the person holding it checks the lead, the intention is a correction. What frequently happens is the opposite. The dog is already anxious; the pressure arrives at the same moment as the trigger, and the whole situation becomes more alarming rather than less. So the dog starts reacting earlier — at twenty metres, then thirty, then forty — getting in first, before the unpleasant thing can happen.

Owners often describe this as the dog “getting worse for no reason”. There is a reason. The pattern was built accidentally, one walk at a time.

Why the usual advice stalls

Flooding. Taking a worried dog to a busy park so it can “get used to it”. A dog that cannot escape does not habituate; it rehearses panic.

Meeting other dogs head-on to say hello. Two dogs on tight leads, face to face, is a genuinely rude greeting in dog terms and an odd thing to ask of a dog that is already unsure.

Punishing the noise. The bark is a warning. Suppress the warning and the fear underneath it is untouched — you have simply removed the part that let you see it coming.

Which one is it?

You can usually narrow it down from home, before anybody assesses anything. Four questions do most of the work.

  • What happens when your dog actually reaches another dog? If it greets, plays, or bounces about, you are very likely looking at frustration. If it stays tense, circles, or the other dog gives it a wide berth, you are not
  • Is it different off the lead? A dog that is civil loose in a field and dreadful on a lead is telling you the lead is part of the problem — frustration, or a dog that knows it cannot get away
  • Does it react to every dog, or a type? Big dark dogs, dogs that stare, small fast ones, entire males. Fear is usually specific. A dog that reacts to absolutely everything that moves is often a dog that is simply over its limit before the walk starts
  • How long does it take to settle afterwards? Seconds, or the rest of the walk? That one answer tells me more about the size of the job than anything else

Hair up, a hard stare and a still body before the noise reads differently from a dog that is bouncing, whining and pulling forward. The first is worried or serious. The second wants to get there.

Distance is the whole thing

Every dog has a distance at which it can see another dog and still think. Outside it, your dog can hear you, take food, and learn something. Inside it, nothing goes in — you are both simply getting through the next thirty seconds.

Almost all the useful work happens outside that distance. That is why the answer to “he was fine until the dog got close” is rarely more practice at close range: it is more practice further out, until the dog can spot a dog and choose to look back at you without being asked.

Owners are often surprised by how far out the line sits at the beginning — the far side of a field, not the far side of a road. It closes, but it closes in metres, not in one session.

Why a bad week undoes a good one

Stress does not reset the moment the other dog has gone. The chemistry of a real fright takes many hours to clear, and sometimes more than a day. So a dog that meets a loose dog on Monday morning is not the same dog on Monday evening, and a dog that has three bad encounters in a week is reacting to things it managed easily last week.

This is why quiet days are part of the plan rather than time off from it. A dog that has had a fright is allowed a boring couple of days: sniffing in a quiet place, work in the garden, a bone. People feel guilty about that. They should not — it is the cheapest progress available.

What to do this week

  • Stop rehearsing it. Change the route, change the hour, drive somewhere quiet. Every scene your dog makes is a repetition of the thing you are trying to undo
  • Learn a clean turn away, practised where there is nothing to turn away from, so it is automatic when there is
  • Use whatever is in the landscape — a parked car, a wall, a gateway, the far verge. Getting behind something is worth ten metres of distance
  • A fixed-length lead, held loose. A tight lead travels straight up the lead to the dog. Retractable leads make both problems worse
  • Feed on the walk, at a distance where your dog will actually eat. The day it stops taking food is the day you know you are too close
  • Say no out loud. “Please call your dog, mine is in training” is a complete sentence, and you do not owe anybody an explanation on a footpath

When a loose dog runs at you

It will happen, and it is the thing owners dread most. What helps, in order: get your dog moving away rather than standing still, keep your own lead loose so your dog is not pinned, put something between them — a car, a gate, your own body — and throw a handful of food sideways past the incoming dog, which surprisingly often redirects it. Shouting at the other owner comes afterwards, if at all.

Carrying something you can drop — a scatter of food, a folded umbrella — is not paranoid. It is the same thinking as a spare tyre.

What the work actually involves

  • Everything starts away from other dogs. A dog that cannot check in with you on an empty lane will not do it with a Labrador bearing down on it
  • An emergency turn, taught to the point of being boring, so it works when you need it in a hurry
  • Look, then look back. At a distance the dog can manage, the other dog becomes the cue to check in with you. Done often enough, that becomes the dog’s own first response instead of the bark
  • A steady helper dog, at a distance we choose, is worth a great deal more than a park full of dogs we do not control
  • Then real life, in steps — a quiet lane, a busier one, a car park, a town — and only when each one is dull
  • A written plan for the bad day, because there will be one

Most owners see meaningful change in weeks and a settled dog in months. The dogs that take longest are not the loudest ones — they are the ones with the longest recovery time.

What success actually looks like

Not a dog that loves other dogs. A dog that can walk past one.

Plenty of dogs finish this work polite and entirely uninterested in other dogs, and that is a perfectly good outcome — it is what most owners wanted in the first place, even if they asked for friendliness. Forcing greetings on a dog that would rather be left alone is how a good result gets undone.

And if your dog has bitten another dog, or would, say so at the start. That changes the plan, usually adds a muzzle taught properly and in advance, and it changes nothing about whether I will take the case — see behaviour consultation.

A dog that barks at other dogs is not a bad dog, and it is very rarely a badly-raised one. It is a dog whose strategy has been working.
