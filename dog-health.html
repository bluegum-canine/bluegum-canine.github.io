Your dog’s health

I am not a vet.
I do know what to look for

A trainer has no business diagnosing your dog, and I will not try. But I spend hours with it, at close quarters, watching how it moves, eats and behaves. I ought to be the first to notice when something is not right, and to know what to do in the minutes before a vet can see it.

Why a trainer needs to know this

Health comes before behaviour. A dog that has started snapping when it is touched may have a sore ear. A dog that has gone back to wetting in the house may have a water infection. A dog that will not settle may be in pain. Training a dog that is unwell is unfair on the dog and a waste of your money, so the first thing I do with a change in behaviour is ask whether a vet should see it before I do.

The second reason is simpler. If your dog stays with me, you are trusting me to look after it as well as train it.

What I am trained to notice

I have been formally taught health and first aid for when I have dogs in my care. It covers the common problems and the true emergencies:

  • A dog that is off. Not eating, quieter than usual, a bit flat. A dog does not have to be lying on its side to be ill
  • Stomach upsets, and the difference between a nervous tummy in a new place and something that needs a vet
  • Coughs, and why a coughing dog is kept away from other dogs straight away
  • Ears, skin and paws. Head shaking, a smell from the ear, a swollen ear flap, hot spots under a collar, a cut pad
  • Trouble passing water, or blood in it
  • Fleas, ticks and worms, and what they carry
  • Overheating. It does not take a hot day or hard work, and small dogs and flat-faced dogs are at most risk
  • Bloat. A swollen belly and a dog trying to be sick with nothing coming up. This one is a straight drive to the vet, with no waiting to see
  • Fits, choking and poisoning, and what to do and not do until help is reached

What I do about it

I watch, I write it down, and I ring you. The same day, not at the end of the week. You decide what happens next, and if I cannot reach you and it will not wait, the vet sees your dog. All of that is agreed with you in writing before a stay begins.

A dog staying with me is checked morning and evening: eyes, ears, coat, paws, how it is moving, what it ate and drank, and what came out the other end. It is not glamorous, but that is where trouble shows first.

At drop-off we go over your dog together, nose to tail, and write down what we find. You sign it, I sign it, and we do it again when you collect. You know exactly what condition your dog arrived in and went home in, and so do I.

In an emergency

I have been taught canine first aid: stopping bleeding, a temporary bandage, cooling an overheated dog safely, what to do for a choking dog, and CPR. First aid buys time to reach the vet. It is not a substitute for one. I know where the nearest vet and the nearest out-of-hours vet are before your dog arrives, and I have your own vet’s number on the sheet.

Ring your vet now, not me. If your dog is at home with you and you see any of these, do not wait and do not look it up. Ring your vet, or the out-of-hours vet, straight away:

  • A swollen, tight belly, and trying to be sick with nothing coming up
  • Straining to pass water with little or nothing coming
  • A fit that lasts more than five minutes, or more than one fit
  • Collapse, staggering or frantic panting in warm weather
  • Something poisonous swallowed: chocolate, grapes or raisins, human tablets, rat bait, anything sweetened with xylitol
  • Struggling to breathe, or gums that have gone pale, grey or blue
  • Bleeding that does not stop with firm pressure

I would rather you rang a vet for nothing than rang me first. Tell me afterwards.

What I will not do

  • Diagnose. I will tell you what I have seen. What it is, is for a vet to say
  • Give your dog anything a vet has not prescribed for it. No leftover tablets, nothing off the internet, no home remedies on somebody else’s dog
  • Wait and see with a dog that is not mine
  • Keep anything from you. If your dog picks up a scratch, a cough or an upset stomach while it is with me, you hear it from me, that day. It is an awkward phone call and I will make it anyway

How you can help

Tell me everything, including the things you have stopped noticing: the ear that flares up every summer, the allergy, the sensitive stomach, the tablets. None of it will stop me working with your dog. All of it changes how I do it.

I have lived and worked with dogs for years, and you do not keep dogs that long without learning what a sick one looks like. A good deal of what is on this page I knew from getting it wrong, or nearly wrong, with my own.

What the Master Trainer programme added was the structure: canine health, disease prevention, kennel hygiene and first aid, taught by people who run a busy training kennel and deal with these things every week.

It does not make me a vet, or a veterinary nurse, and I will never present myself as either. It makes me a trainer who knows what normal looks like, notices when a dog is not, and knows who to ring.
