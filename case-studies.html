Case studies

The work,
written up

Every case I take gets written up properly: what the dog was doing, what was actually causing it, what we did about it, how long it really took, and where the dog ended up — including the parts that did not go to plan.

This is unusual, and it is deliberate. Almost nobody in this trade publishes outcomes. You will find testimonials everywhere — a sentence of praise with a first name attached — and almost no record of what was done or how long it really took.

Testimonials tell you a client was pleased. A case study lets you judge the work.

It is also what Sharon and I would want to read before letting a stranger near one of our own dogs, which is the test I am holding this page to. There are two cases on it so far, both labelled honestly for what they are. There will be more from January, written the same way.

Trained at Highland Canine · August 2026

The dog with nothing
to pay her with

A stray Doodle with a bite history, who would not take food and would not play. Seven days to a reliable off‑lead recall with people and dogs in the field.

Chloe was picked up as a stray, so there was no history to work from — only that she had bitten people before she arrived. She was assigned to me for the week — my dog to assess, my plan, and my work on the line at the end of it.

The bite history was not what made her hard. This was:

She would not take food. Not “preferred something better” — she would not eat from me at all. And she would not play. No ball, no tug, no chase.

Nearly every recall you have ever seen taught is built on one of those two things. Chloe had handed me an empty list. There was nothing to pay her with.

If you have been told your dog is “not food motivated” and “doesn’t play”, this is the case to read. That is almost never the whole story.

What was actually going on

A dog that will not eat in front of you is not a dog without an appetite. Appetite is one of the first things to switch off under stress, and it comes back when the stress comes down. Play is the same signal further along the line — play is what a dog does when it feels safe enough to be silly.

So the problem was not that Chloe had no drives. The problem was that I was not yet a person she could eat or play in front of. Her reinforcers were not missing. They were switched off, and I was part of what was switching them off.

That decided the order of the whole week. You cannot train a recall with a reward you do not have — so getting her eating and playing was not a warm-up before the training started. It was the first piece of training.

The plan

Two strands, run together rather than one after the other.

  1. 01

    Take the pressure off

    No walking straight at her, no reaching over her, no leaning in, no holding eye contact. Everything sideways and at an angle. Same handling, same voice, same routine every single time — for a frightened dog, being predictable is itself a reward.

  2. 02

    Let her choose the distance

    Every approach was hers to make, and nothing unpleasant ever happened at the end of one. That is the whole foundation of a recall: coming to me had to be the safest decision available to her.

  3. 03

    Get the rewards back online

    Food came back first. Play came back after food — and once it did, it raised the ceiling on everything, because play travels into a distracting environment better than food does and it does not fill a dog up.

  4. 04

    Then build the recall properly

    Close range first, where it could not fail. A silent hand signal as the cue — no verbal command at any point. Distance added on a long line, a bit at a time. A sit in front on arrival, built in from the start rather than bolted on. Every single correct recall paid.

Why both, and not just one

This is the part worth taking away. The training could not have started without the relationship, because there was no reward to train with. And the relationship on its own would not have finished it — a dog that likes you is not the same thing as a dog that turns and comes back on a hand signal at forty feet with another dog in the field.

Trust made the learning possible. The training made it reliable. Take either one away and this case fails, for a reason you can put your finger on.

How long it took

Seven days. And the shape of it matters more than the number: the trust work took most of the week, and the recall itself came together quickly once there was something to pay her with.

That looks like a slow start. It is not. The days spent getting Chloe eating and playing were the days the recall was being built — because they were the days that made a recall trainable at all.

Where she ended up

At the end of the week Chloe gave a reliable recall off the lead, with other people and other dogs around her in the field. The exam asked for less than that — a hand-signal recall on a long line, between fifteen and forty feet. She did it loose, and under distraction, with people in view. People being the thing she had bitten.

The photograph on the About page is her, at the end of that week. I am fonder of that dog than a week is supposed to make you.

What this case does not claim

Chloe’s bite history was not resolved in seven days, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. What changed is that she gained a trained behaviour that gets her out of trouble and gives a handler control at distance without a lead. That is a real safety outcome. It is not the same as saying she is fixed.

And seven days is a facility timeline — a dog kennelled on site, a fixed date to be ready for, structured access every day. Your dog and your week do not look like that, and I will not quote you seven days on the strength of this.

Trained at Highland Canine · September 2026

The window that
closed too early

A two‑year‑old Great Dane who had never been properly socialised, and who had learned that a growl makes people go away. Several weeks of desensitisation — most of it done by her owner, not by me.

Stevie is a Great Dane, two years old, and she missed her socialisation almost entirely. Nothing dramatic happened to her. That is the point of the case: nothing had to.

By the time I met her, this is what she was doing. Anyone who caught her eye got a growl and bared teeth. Not a warning that built slowly — eye contact was the trigger, and the response came straight off the back of it. She could not be approached by a stranger at all, and she could not be walked safely on a lead anywhere with people in it.

She weighs 175 lb, and the growl that comes with her is deep enough to stop a grown adult mid‑stride. People read all of that as a dog about to do something. Almost none of it was.

A dog that growls at eye contact is usually asking for distance, not threatening to close it. The size changes how frightening it looks. It does not change what the dog is saying.

What was actually going on

There is a stretch early in a puppy’s life when new things are filed as normal more or less automatically. Strangers, hats, traffic, children, other dogs, hands reaching in — a puppy who meets all of that in the right window comes out the far side with a bank of evidence that says none of it matters. Stevie did not get that window. She arrived at adulthood with an empty bank.

So a stranger looking at her was not a neutral event she had two hundred examples of. It was new, and in dog terms a direct stare from something unfamiliar is pressure. Her answer to pressure was the only one she had.

And here is the part that had made it worse every week for two years: it worked. She growled, the person backed off, the pressure stopped. That is a behaviour being paid, reliably, several times a day, by strangers who had no idea they were training her. She was not getting more aggressive. She was getting better rehearsed.

The plan

Change what a stranger predicts, before asking her to do anything about strangers at all.

  1. 01

    Take eye contact off the table

    Nobody stares at her, nobody walks straight at her, nobody reaches over her head. The single thing that set her off was the easiest thing in the world to stop doing, and stopping it bought us a dog who could think.

  2. 02

    Work at a distance she can cope at

    Close enough to notice a person, far enough not to react to one. That distance is the whole dial. Everything after this is moving it in by a few feet at a time and being willing to move it back out again on a bad day.

  3. 03

    Pay the stranger, not the behaviour

    Food appears because a person appeared — not because Stevie did anything correct. That is deliberate. We are not training a sit near strangers, we are changing what a stranger means. The day she started taking food with people in view was the day we knew the stress had come down far enough to learn.

  4. 04

    Hand it to the owner

    Short, frequent, boring repetitions on ordinary walks, every day, done properly. The sessions set the homework up. The homework is what actually moved her.

Why the owner’s half matters more than mine

Worth being blunt about this, because it is the part clients underestimate. Private one‑to‑one sessions were a small fraction of the hours that changed this dog. Two years of rehearsal does not get outvoted in an hour a week.

Desensitisation is a repetitions‑over‑time business. It happens on the school run and the evening walk, in the gap between sessions, done by the person holding the lead. Stevie’s owner did that work — consistently, and at the boring end of it, which is the hard part. A trainer who tells you otherwise is selling you their own hours.

How long it took

Several weeks, and it is not finished. I am not going to round that up into a number that sounds better. A fear history built over two years is not unpicked on a schedule, and anybody quoting you one for this kind of case is guessing.

Where she is now

She is not growling at people. She is taking treats. She will allow herself to be patted. And she can be walked past people without reacting to them, which is the one her owner actually wanted, because it is the difference between a dog you can take out and a dog you cannot.

Every one of those is the same underlying change showing up in a different place: strangers stopped predicting pressure.

That clip is the whole case in twelve seconds, and it is worth saying what to look at. The line is long but it is slack — nothing is being held together by my grip. There are four people on the road ahead of her, walking towards us. She is looking at them. And she keeps walking.

A few weeks earlier, any one of those people glancing at her would have got a growl.

What this case does not claim

Stevie is not a finished dog and we have some distance to go. What has changed is her default. The old one is still in there, and under the wrong combination — cornered, startled, a stranger who reaches in over the top of her — it is still the answer she reaches for first. That is not a failure of the work. It is what a fear history is.

Her owner still manages her rather than assuming her. That is the correct way to hold a dog like this, and it does not stop being correct because the growling stopped.

And the obvious one, which is the reason this case is on the site at all: none of this would have been necessary. Socialisation at the formative stages should never be underestimated. It is a handful of weeks of ordinary, unglamorous exposure while a puppy is young, and the alternative is what you have just read — several weeks of careful work, ongoing, to get back to a starting line she should never have been behind.
